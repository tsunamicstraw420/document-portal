Understand AI filmmaking

Plan the film and write production-ready prompts before you generate anything.

Level 1 · Bronze — "AI Screenwriter"
01What you'll build in 7 daysThe three stages every film goes throughTo do›
Goal: understand why a film is built in stages, not with one button.

The big idea

  • One sentence typed into an AI tool gives you one okay clip. It does not give you a film.
  • A film needs a plan, a character who looks the same in every shot, and shots that connect.
  • Every film goes through the same three stages, always in this order: pre-production (plan), production (make), post-production (finish).
  • Beginners skip planning and jump to generating. That is why their results feel random. The quality is decided while planning.

How AI video really works

The AI doesn't film anything. It has learned from millions of real videos how faces move, how light falls and how cameras move. It then makes new frames that follow those patterns. A clear instruction gets an intentional result. A vague one gets a random result.

A sharp, well-lit, front-facing starting picture gives the AI clear information. A blurry one makes faces flicker and shift.

What you finish with

  • A finished short film you can post or show a client.
  • A repeatable workflow, so the second film is faster.
  • A trailer, which is your best portfolio piece.
The goal this week is finished, not perfect.

03Master promptThe five-element formula and your Prompt BankTo do›
Goal: stop writing one-line prompts. Write a structured brief with five parts.

Why one line fails

"A woman walking in an office" only names a subject. The AI has to invent the look, the camera, the light and the mood. When the AI guesses, the result looks like nobody's.

The five elements

ElementAnswersVague → specific
SubjectWho or what is in frame?"a woman" → "a woman in her late 30s in a dark green coat"
ActionWhat are they doing, and where?"some action" → "walks slowly through frame, head down"
StyleWhat's the overall look? One word is enough."cool style" → "noir, high contrast, desaturated"
CameraHow is it framed, and does it move?"interesting angle" → "low angle looking up, slow push-in"
LightWhat quality, direction and mood?"nice lighting" → "soft golden-hour light from the left"

Four or five short sentences is plenty. Aim for one element per sentence.

Five-element skeleton 
[Subject — specific: age, look, clothing]. [Action — what they do and where]. Look: [one style word]. Camera: [shot type and movement]. Light: [quality, direction and mood].

Practice: spot what's missing

  • "A man in a grey suit crosses a rooftop. Wide shot, slow drift left. Film noir." → missing Light.
  • "A harbour village at golden hour, boats drifting in. Documentary style." → missing Camera.
  • "Hands kneading dough, window light from the left, extreme close-up pulling back." → missing Style.

04Pre-productionRefine the script and lock the visual styleTo do›
Goal: leave this lesson with a final script, a visual style description and a full set of prompts.

The planning pipeline

  1. Pick a topic and run the script starter.
  2. Shape the script: scene descriptions and any voiceover written together.
  3. Define the visual style: colours, light, texture and references. This is the "DNA" that keeps every shot consistent.
  4. Use the five-element formula.
  5. Have Claude turn script + style into one prompt per scene (your Prompt Bank).

Each step's output is the next step's input.

Direct Claude like a director

Change one thing at a time. Stay in the same chat so Claude remembers the script. Run at least one of these:

Sharper hook 
The opening hook is too slow. Rewrite only the hook so a single frame would stop someone scrolling. Leave everything else as it is.
Change one scene's mood 
Make Beat 2 feel tense and uneasy instead of calm. Keep the same place and the same action — change only the emotional tone.
One voiceover line 
Add a single voiceover line to the closing shot that captures what the whole video is about. Make it poetic, not explanatory.

Define the visual style

Visual style 
Using the script above, define the visual style of this film:
1. Colour palette — the 3 or 4 main colours across all scenes
2. Light — warm or cool, soft or hard, natural or artificial
3. Texture — clean and sharp, or grainy and organic
4. References — 2 or 3 real films, photographers or styles it should feel like
5. One sentence that sums up the look

Be specific. I will use this to keep every generated frame consistent.

05Shot languageLenses, camera moves, light and one style anchorTo do›
Goal: write each shot like a director's note to a camera operator, not "make it look good."

The five parts of a shot (in this order)

  1. Subject and action — who's in frame and what they do.
  2. Camera spec — lens, angle, framing, how blurry the background is.
  3. Camera motion — how the camera moves, and for how long.
  4. Light and atmosphere — time of day, quality of light, what's in the air.
  5. Style anchor — one reference that fixes the overall look.

Beginners describe the subject and stop. Camera motion and light are what make a clip look filmed rather than generated.

Lenses

LensEffectUse for
24mm wideLots in frame, exaggerated depthEstablishing shots, landscapes, dramatic low angles
35mmNatural, slightly wide, documentaryWalk-and-talks, handheld street scenes
35mm anamorphicWidescreen, horizontal flaresAnything that should feel like a feature film
50mmClose to the human eyeCharacter close-ups, dialogue
80mm telephotoFlattened depth, isolated subjectSport, action, a lonely figure
90–100mm macroExtreme detailObjects, textures, product shots

Add "shallow depth of field" or "f/1.8" to blur the background. Add "deep focus" to keep it sharp.

Camera moves (name the move and its length)

  • Tracking from behind — follows the subject, builds momentum.
  • Slow dolly forward over 5 seconds — pulls the viewer in.
  • Drone push forward — opens up a landscape.
  • Top-down push-in — hands and objects.
  • Focus pull from background to subject — shifts attention.
  • Slight handheld — feels human and real.
  • Crane up — reveals scale at the end.
  • Static with small facial movements — for faces. Stillness is a choice.

Always give a lens and a move. Add an angle (low, top-down, over-the-shoulder, side) only when it matters to the story.

Light

  • Always name the time of day. Without it you get flat midday light, the least cinematic option.
  • Warm and nostalgic: golden hour, amber, soft tungsten.
  • Tense: low-key light, hard side light, deep shadows, high contrast.
  • Clean and commercial: soft diffused studio light, no harsh shadows.
  • Intimate: soft window light.
  • Cold: cool blue tones, teal shadows.
  • Give the light something to travel through: mist, rain, steam, dust in a beam, lens flare, film grain.
  • In a cool scene, one warm accent (a single street lamp) keeps it from looking dead.
One style anchor, not three. "Cinematic, anime, photorealistic" asks for three films at once, and the model picks one at random.

Example shot, all five parts

Example 
A tired delivery rider in a yellow rain jacket pushes his bike up a steep wet street at night. Tracking shot from behind, 35mm lens, eye-level, slight handheld. Low-key light, cool blue tones, one warm shop sign glowing on the right, light rain catching the light. Slow dolly forward over 5 seconds. Cinematic, 24fps, film grain.

Picture first, then video

Typing a scene straight into a video model makes the AI invent everything at once. Every retry costs video credits. In the course's example, it produced the wrong character entirely.

The professional way takes two steps:

  1. Make a still picture. It's cheap and fast. Check the face, light and framing. Fix the prompt and remake until it's right.
  2. Animate that picture. Upload it to video. The prompt now only describes motion and camera, e.g. @image1 walks forward cautiously, slow push-in, quiet forest sounds.
07Save credits, get resultsThree rules and what things costTo do›

Three rules

  1. Two variations at most. Pick the better one, then improve the prompt. Don't fire off five and hope.
  2. Test video at 6 seconds. That's enough to judge a shot, at about half the cost of 10 seconds. Make the full length only once it works.
  3. Keep everything. Rejected takes become filler shots, transitions and backgrounds later.

09Defeat the AI lookTexture, imperfection and a pre-animation checkTo do›
Goal: pictures that look photographed, not like wax figures.

Why AI looks fake

AI makes everything too perfect. Real photos have pores, small asymmetries and texture. Viewers sense the difference even when they can't name it.

Five habits (mostly before you generate)

  1. Ask for texture, not beauty. "Light freckles, visible pores, a slightly uneven smile" makes a person. "Beautiful woman" makes wax.
  2. Break the symmetry. A strand of hair on one side, one eyebrow higher, weight on one leg.
  3. Light like a photographer. Name a direction and quality: "soft window light from camera left, deep shadow on the far cheek." Flat front light reads as AI.
  4. Pick the model for the actual failure. Faces: Nano Banana. Materials (fabric, glass, water, skin under light): Seedream.
  5. Check at full size. Hands, eyes and background artifacts hide in thumbnails.

12Your first videoTimeline prompts and @tagsTo do›

Timeline prompt = set-up + timed beats

6-second template 
[Set-up: @image1 as the character, the place from @image2, light and mood]

0–2 s: [what happens] — [what the camera does]
2–4 s: [what happens next] — [camera]
4–6 s: [how it ends, character in a clear still pose] — [camera holds]

Ambient sound: [e.g. quiet forest, wind, no dialogue]

For 15 seconds, use five 3-second beats: opening, development, key moment, build or resolution, final pose.

13Advanced controlStart/end frames and seamless chainingTo do›
Goal: join clips so the viewer never sees where one ends and the next begins.

Start and end frames

  • Start frame: the clip opens on exactly this picture.
  • End frame: the clip has to arrive at this picture. The model works out the path.
  • Both: the most control. Good for walking across a room, a wide-to-close move, or calm turning to alarmed.

These are different from reference images. References say what someone looks like. Start and end frames say exactly how the clip begins and ends.

Seamless chaining: the most important production skill

The last frame of clip A becomes the start frame of clip B.

  1. Make clip A.
  2. Grab its last frame (pause at the end and screenshot it).
  3. Put that frame in clip B's start-frame slot.
  4. Write clip B's prompt starting from that exact pose.
  5. Generate, then play A and B back to back.
Clip B template 
@image1 continues from the pose in the start frame. Location from @image2.

0–1 s: Character holds the exact pose from the start frame. No movement. Camera matches the start frame's angle.
1–4 s: [next action]. [camera move].
4–6 s: [action, ending on a still pose]. Camera holds.

Four habits for clean joins

  • End every clip on a still moment (seated, standing, looking), never mid-step.
  • Keep the camera still at the join. Start moving again after a beat.
  • Load the last frame as the start frame, and the whole of clip A as a video reference.
  • Write the one-second hold into the prompt. Don't assume the model will do it.
The start frame gets you about 90% of the way. Making 2–3 takes and picking the smoothest closes the rest. That's normal practice, not a workaround.

Task

14Make your character speakLipsync, voices, emotion and sound directionTo do›
Goal: a talking clip where lips, voice and expression match.

Who does what

The audio file carries the words. The prompt carries the performance: posture, gestures, camera. Don't type the dialogue into the prompt. Refer to @audio1 instead. The model matches mouth shapes to sounds and reads the emotion in the voice.

Get a voice track

  • Record yourself: quiet room, natural pace, a one-second pause at each end, .mp3 or .wav.
  • Studio text-to-speech (7ART uses ElevenLabs): about 10 credits per 1,000 characters, so it's very cheap.
  • The model's own voice: some models (e.g. Seedance) speak dialogue you give as text.

Dialogue clip

Talking-clip template 
@image1 sits at a table in the place from @image2, speaking to camera, synced to @audio1.

0–3 s: Neutral look into the lens, then starts speaking. Slight head tilt. Medium close-up, camera holds.
3–6 s: Speaks with growing intensity, leans in a little, one hand gesture on the table. Slow push-in.

Check the result for:

  • Lips: especially on m, b and p sounds.
  • Emotion: a tense voice should get a tense face.
  • Life: small head moves and blinks, not frozen from the neck down.

Automatic emotion, and when to override

The model reads tone from the voice:

  • Tension: furrowed brow, tight jaw.
  • Excitement: raised eyebrows, more movement.
  • Sadness: lowered gaze, slower movement.
  • Anger: narrowed eyes, sharp movements.

For a specific beat ("calm, then shocked at 4 s") or a wrong guess, write it in that time step, e.g. "serious, no smile, tight jaw." What you write beats what the model guesses.

Clips without dialogue still need sound direction

Ambient sound lines 
Ambient sound: distant city traffic, soft wind, no dialogue.
Ambient sound: quiet forest, birdsong, footsteps on dry leaves.
Ambient sound: busy café, cups clinking, low murmur, no clear voices.

Without one, models (Veo in particular) may invent random noise or speech.

Current limits

  • Lip accuracy drops after 10–12 seconds. Split monologues into 6–8 second pieces.
  • Radio, echo or phone effects go on in the edit. Generate clean dialogue.
  • Never put music in the voice file. Music goes on in the edit.
  • One speaker per clip. For a conversation, shoot each person's lines separately and cut between them.
  • Singing is less reliable. Test early.

15Video editingCapCut basics, splicing and five editing rulesTo do›
Goal: get set up in CapCut and learn how editors think.

Set up

  • Install the free CapCut desktop app from capcut.com (the web version works too). Start a New Project.
  • Three areas: media panel (top left), preview (top right), timeline (bottom).
  • Shortcuts: Space = play/pause. Cmd+B = split at the playhead. Cmd+Z = undo. Cmd+scroll = zoom the timeline.
  • Import your final clips, music, ambient sound and voice files.

Splicing two chained clips

Put clip B right after clip A with no gap and play across the join. If you can see a jump, try these in order:

  1. Trim to the best match: go frame by frame and cut both clips where the pose matches best.
  2. Tiny dissolve: 3–5 frames (about 0.1–0.2 s). Too short to notice, but it hides small jumps.
  3. Cutaway: slip in 1–2 seconds of hands, a detail or a reaction to cover the join.

Five editing rules

  • Every cut is a decision. If you can't say why you're cutting, don't.
  • Clip length sets the mood: 4–6 s gives space, 2–4 s carries dialogue and story, 1–2 s creates urgency. Move in a wave: slow → medium → fast → medium → slow.
  • Start late, end early. Drop the still frames at each end.
  • Sound leads the cut: a J-cut starts the next scene's sound 0.5–1 s before its picture. An L-cut lets this scene's sound run over the next picture.
  • 180° rule: keep the camera on one side of the action. If someone faces right, they keep facing right.
16Full editTrim, transitions, three audio layers, exportTo do›
Goal: a tight cut with clean sound, exported at full quality.

Trim every clip

Clip typeKeep in the edit
Establishing wide3–5 s

DialogueThe speech + 0.5 s

Filler / transition2–3 s
Final shot3–5 s

Use split (Cmd+B) for precise cuts. CapCut never touches your original files, so cut boldly.

Only three transitions

  • Hard cut: about 90% of cuts.
  • Cross-dissolve (0.5–1 s): only when time passes or the place changes. Never between two shots of the same scene.
  • Fade to black (1–2 s): the end, or after a heavy moment.

Transitions on every cut make a film look like a slideshow.

Three sound layers

  1. Dialogue: loudest. Use Noise reduction if it's rough.
  2. Ambient sound: about 20–30% of dialogue volume.
  3. Music: about 10–20%. Fade in 1 s before the start and out over the last 2–3 s.

If you can't hear the words over the music, the music is too loud. Try one J-cut: unlink the audio and drag it 0.5–1 s earlier.

Export

  • The highest resolution your clips support:
    • YouTube: 1080p or 4K.
    • Vertical: 1080×1920.
    • Client or portfolio: 4K.
  • MP4.
  • Frame rate: 24 fps for story and drama, 30 fps for ads and tutorials.
  • Highest quality setting, and leave the audio settings alone.
  • Watch the exported file once before uploading anywhere.

CapCut also has templates, auto-captions, text-to-speech, background removal and colour correction.

17Post-production tipsHabits that save the editTo do›
Goal: rescue near-misses cheaply and know when to reshoot.

Habits

  • Trim about half a second off both ends of every clip. AI drifts most at the edges.
  • Cut on movement (a blink, a head turn), never on a held still frame.
  • If a clip feels cheap, check the sound first: room tone, one layer of sound effects on the main action, music at 20–30% of dialogue.
  • Generate big, crop later: a 4K clip can be cropped into a second, tighter 1080p angle for free.
  • Keep every take.

Why a trailer

Few people watch a stranger's 60-second film. Almost everyone watches 20 seconds. A trailer proves you can hook an audience, and that's what clients pay for. It isn't a shortened film. Its job is to create desire, not to explain.

Trailer shape

  • 0–3 s, hook: one image that interrupts the scroll.
  • 3–12 s, tension: fragments, faster cuts, rising music.
  • 12–18 s, reveal: one peak moment, line or image.
  • 18–22 s, title card: clean and bold.

No explaining, no introductions. Leave them with questions.

Three formulas

  • The Question: open on a provocative question and never answer it. Good for sci-fi and thriller.
  • The Countdown: each cut is shorter than the last. The easiest first trailer.
  • The Mood Piece: no dialogue, slow cuts, music carries it. Good for nature, travel and emotional stories.
20-second trailer + all prompts 
Here is the full script of my short AI film:
[PASTE SCRIPT]

Write a 20-second trailer using the Countdown formula (start slow, every cut shorter than the last):
Shot 1 (0–4 s), Shot 2 (4–7 s), Shot 3 (7–9 s), Shot 4 (9–10.5 s), Shot 5 (10.5–11.5 s), Shot 6 (11.5–12 s), Shot 7 (12–15 s: title on black).
For each: what's on screen and what the camera does.

Rules: never give away the ending; shot 1 must stop a stranger scrolling with no context; no dialogue except at most one line under 8 words; only what the camera sees.

Then for each shot give:
1. An image prompt (subject, action, place, light, camera)
2. A timeline video prompt using @image1 if it moves — or mark it [STILL] if it works better as a held frame under music

Save the answer as your Trailer Prompt Bank. If one part is off, give one targeted note ("hook more visual, no dialogue") rather than re-running.

Vertical short drama means episodes of 60–90 seconds, each with one turn and ending on a cliffhanger.

How the series studio works (7ART Short Drama Generator)

  1. Give it something to start from: a one-line premise, an existing script, or up to 24,000 characters of text.
  2. Answer up to 6 questions about genre, setting and situation, or let the AI decide.
  3. You get back:
    • A logline for the whole series.
    • An outline: a goal and an emotional beat per episode.
    • A cliffhanger chain.
    • Full scripts.
  4. Re-run the outline and cliffhangers until they're right. They're cheap. The renders are where the money goes.
  5. Cast and world: up to 12 characters, 30 places and 30 props. Each is locked for the whole season once approved.
  6. Each scene renders as one generation with its own internal cuts. You can edit the scene prompt before rendering.
  7. Autopilot scripts and shoots one episode at a time. It pauses when credits run out and picks up where it left off.

Costs and limits (per the course)

  • A look image on Nano Banana 2 at 2K: 24 credits.
  • A Seedance 2.5 scene: 37 credits per second at 480p, 79 at 720p.
  • Up to 12 episodes × 8 scenes. A 6-episode series is 48 renders.
  • Start with 3–4 episodes and check the series estimate first.
  • Sound is off by default. Add voice, music, sound effects and lipsync afterwards.

You can export the series bible, the scripts, the prompts and the character, place and prop sheets. That package is a strong portfolio piece, and it can also be sold as a template.
