   Choosing between models
   Nano Banana 2 (Gemini 3.1 Flash Image, Feb 2026) is the newer model and handles most everyday jobs. It
   offers near-Pro quality at higher speed, reliable text rendering, and the strongest multi-reference
   consistency. It is the default for editing, product and brand work.

   Nano Banana Pro (Gemini 3 Pro Image, Nov 2025) is the premium pick when you need accuracy, brand
   precision, or when refining the most difficult prompts. It takes longer time to process but typically reaches
   a higher end result in fidelity. The two cost the same per image, so route by behaviour, not price.

   GPT Image 2 is best for complex layout-focused designs.

   Recraft earns its slot for typographic dependent posters and art-school level illustration, and is the only
   current option that exports vector files.

   Flux.2 is built for control. It accepts up to ten references at once, matches exact hex colours, and follows
   structured, shot-list prompts, which suits locked-down brand and product pipelines. Give it an explicit
   brief; a vague prompt returns a polished but generic image.

   Krea 2 is built for style. It works from style references and moodboards and produces looks that avoid the
   default AI polish. The trade-off is precision: Krea 2 interprets the brief rather than following it exactly, and it
   is weak at in-image text.

Image generation
How you write the prompt now matters more than any quality tags you add: describe a coherent
scene and the model follows it. The reasoning models (Nano Banana Pro and 2, GPT Image 2, and
the Seedream line) read a busy prompt in parts before rendering, so they hold up when a scene
has many subjects or a lot of detail.

Field notes: killing the slop
Left alone, every model drifts toward the same default: subject centred and staring at the lens,
posed and evenly lit, everything in focus, with a processed gloss creeping in on top. There's a word
for it now, and your audience knows it: slop. People clock it in half a second, and it tells them a
brand didn't care enough to look. Never ship it. Slop is the default, so every parameter you set (the
framing, the moment, the lens, the time of day, the light) moves the image one step away from it;
every choice you leave open, the model fills with its most average answer. The fix is two habits:
direct the shot (off-centre framing, a real moment, depth) and then finish the grade toward soft
cinematic glass: gentle contrast, creamy focus roll-off, fine uniform grain. The two blocks below
handle the grade; the framing is on you to name.


   THE FILM-LOOK LINE                                            THE SKIN-REALISM BLOCK

   soft cinematic lens, shallow depth of field                   Natural skin texture: realistic pores, fine
   with creamy focus roll-off, gentle contrast                   hairs, subtle veining, a faint highlight.
   with soft highlight roll-off, fine uniform                    Tactile and real, not retouched plastic.
   film grain, no oversharpening, no digital
   look




Banned words. These summon the slop. Don't write them: ultra sharp · hyper detailed · crisp · razor sharp · 8K
clarity · HDR · ultra-realistic detail. Grain is the one film artifact worth keeping; never dust, scratches or “old
damaged film.”

Field notes: controlling the frame
The model fills every gap you leave open. These four habits close the gaps that pull a frame off-
brief most often.


Word leakage                        A mood word colours the whole frame, not just the thing it describes.
                                    Write “dewy” or “sensual” anywhere, even about the lighting, and the
                                    model nudges the entire image toward suggestive: clothing starts
                                    slipping and expressions turn pouty. Counter it with explicit
                                    instructions, even when they feel unnecessary. Straps stay neatly in
                                    place; lips are closed and relaxed.


Negative prompts                    Keep a standing ban list for the usual drift and paste it into every
                                    portrait prompt: smiling, glamour makeup, jewelry, plastic smooth
                                    skin, dramatic rim light, colored background, soft focus. Escalate only
                                    when needed: no pouting, no parted lips.


Spell out stubborn words            When rendered text keeps mangling a word (“FOIUS” for FOCUS),
                                    restate it letter by letter in the edit prompt: “spelled F, O, C, U, S”, and
                                    it lands. The “SIGNAL” program poster earlier in this part was fixed
                                    exactly this way.


Character sheets                    For recurring characters, one three-panel sheet becomes the master
                                    reference every later generation is built from. It earns its own section,
                                    on the next page.

Character sheets
For recurring characters nothing pays off more: build a three-panel character sheet before a
character appears twice anywhere, and make it the master reference. Every later generation takes
the sheet along as a reference; every prompt describing the character is written from it. When the
identity already exists, start from a reference portrait, as here; when it doesn't, generate the sheet
from text.
FIELD EXAMPLE · CHARACTER SHEET · NANO BANANA 2 · FROM A REFERENCE




The idea: turn one portrait into the character's official ID: straight-on, side profile, full body in the final
wardrobe, all on one sheet. From here on, the sheet rides along as a reference in every generation, and every
prompt describing her is written from it.

  PROMPT

  reference: her portrait · Character sheet: one 16:9 frame divided into three equal panels on a
  pure white studio background. Left panel: straight-on head-and-shoulders portrait. Centre panel:
  exact side profile portrait. Right panel: full body, standing relaxed. The same woman in all
  three panels, the woman from the reference image, keeping her identity exactly: dark hair
  slicked back, magenta acetate sunglasses, natural lips, blue technical shell jacket with orange
  trim details, matching blue trousers and clean sneakers. Soft even studio light, no props, no
  text, fine film grain, photorealistic.



When the character changes, the sheet changes first. New wardrobe, new haircut, new age: edit the sheet,
not the shots. Regenerate it once with the change described, then keep prompting from the new sheet; a
character has exactly one current sheet at any time.

Features & key concepts
Six capabilities define the omni generation of video engines, and the rest of this part builds on
them. Two of the six are confused so often that they get their own comparison below.


Multi-modal (“omni”)                 One model ingests text, images, video and audio together and
                                     outputs video with sound in a single pass. This single-pass design is
                                     the biggest change from the previous generation of video engines,
                                     which took a text prompt and returned silent picture.


Native audio                         Dialogue, effects and ambient sound are generated alongside the
                                     picture and locked to it, rather than added in post, including lip-
                                     synced speech.


Start / end frame                    Supply a first frame (and optionally a last) and the model animates
                                     the motion between them, giving precise control over how a shot
                                     opens and lands.


References                           Image references fix a character or product; video references teach
                                     motion and voice; audio references set a voice or soundscape.


Multi-shot storyboarding             Several cuts are generated in one job with continuity, each shot
                                     carrying its own prompt for duration, framing and action.


Character consistency                “Elements” and coreference lock identities so multiple characters stay
                                     distinct and on-model across every shot and scene change.

Each model orders it slightly differently. Seedance: state shots, duration and aspect ratio up front, then
subject → action → environment → camera → style, labelling references as @image1 / @video1 / @audio1. Kling:
subject + movement + scene + camera + lighting + atmosphere. Happy Horse follows the same shot-description
discipline. Gemini Omni Flash is the exception: it is tuned for conversational direction, so you can prompt
loosely and refine over follow-up turns.

  Front-load the shot. Open with the shot type and one camera move; the model reads the first line first.
  One action, one camera move per clip. Stacking either produces warping and instability.
  Use real cinematography language. “Slow dolly-in,” named light sources, exact shot sizes. Concrete moves and
  light beat mood words, and lens numbers barely register.

Field notes: engine rules
Video engines share a set of behaviours worth designing around. They can sound like superstition;
treat them as law. Every rule here earned its place the first time a render broke it.

  COMPANION SPACE · Open the directing canvas to run every example in this part live.




Chaining clips: the five seams

A multi-clip film is a chain of seams, and each seam answers one question: does the next clip
continue this frame, continue this motion, pass through something, or break clean?

Seam                What it is

Frozen handoff      The last frame of clip N becomes the first frame of clip N+1: end on a freezable state and restate
                    it exactly. This is the seam the pipeline on the previous page runs.

Action bridge       A motion starts at the end of one clip and completes at the start of the next from a new angle.
                    Name the motion and its direction in both prompts.

Match cut           Two frames share a shape, position or colour while the scene changes. Name the rhyming
                    element in both prompts.

Portal              The camera pushes into something that fills the frame (a pupil, steam, a doorway) and emerges
                    somewhere new. Ration it: one per film, or it reads as a gimmick.

Hard cut            A clean break; montage logic. The default when nothing needs to connect.

The brief decides the seam. “Seamless / one take / it should flow” → frozen handoff · “cut to / meanwhile /
vignettes” → hard cuts · “morphs into / becomes” → match cut. When the brief is silent, hard cut is the default;
never portal.

Two rules on top. Work from what rendered, not what was planned: before writing the next clip, extract the
actual final frame and rewrite the opening against it. And lock the product: repeat its description word-for-word
in every clip's prompt; that repetition is what keeps it consistent across separate generations. Keep chained
clips to 30 seconds each.

Twelve camera moves
Cinematographers learn these in film school; most AI creators never name one, so the camera just
sits there. Name the move and the shot comes alive.

Move                                  What it does

Push-in                               Moves toward the subject. Builds tension and intimacy; keep it slow.

Pull-back                             Retreats to reveal. Isolation, scale, endings: the reveal shot.

Pan                                   Horizontal rotation, camera fixed. Suspense lives in what you haven't
                                      shown yet.

Tilt                                  The vertical pan. Tilt up on a hero and they read as powerful.

Tracking                              Camera travels with the subject. Energy, forward motion, “you're
                                      there.”

Arc / orbit                           Circles the subject. A product on a clean pedestal takes a full turn;
                                      keep arcs around people under ~30°.

Crane / jib                           Sweeps vertically on a boom. Grandeur and scale: the god-view.

Zoom                                  Focal length changes, camera stays put. Flatter than a dolly; fast
                                      zoom = music-video energy.

Dolly zoom                            Camera and lens move opposite ways. Background warps, subject
                                      holds: pure dread.

Whip pan / crash zoom                 Extreme speed for transitions. Shock, comedy, scroll-stopping.

Handheld                              Natural, unstabilised shake. Add “subtle” or the model goes full
                                      earthquake.

Static + angles                       Low, high, Dutch, bird's-eye, worm's-eye. Low = power; Dutch =
                                      unease; bird's-eye = scale.

The mistake everyone makes is stacking moves into one prompt. One move, one clip, always. And add “slow”
to almost everything: slow moves hide what the model can't render cleanly; fast moves expose every flaw.
