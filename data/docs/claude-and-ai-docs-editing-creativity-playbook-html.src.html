Editor's Visual Playbook

Matching picture to narration, line by line. Compiled from documentary & video-essay editing practice, Walter Murch, and retention-editing research. Every rule is written to be executed.

Core reframe. The old constraint was "we only have generic B-roll, so we reuse it." That constraint is gone. Because we can now generate a specific still for almost any line, one at a time, the default answer to "what goes here?" is now generate the exact thing being said — not reach for a generic clip. Treat every reuse of generic filler as a deliberate choice you have to justify, not the fallback.

1 Deciding what goes on screen for each sentence

  • The B-Roll Grid — lay every visual decision on two axes. Horizontal = story progression (a sequential chain of shots showing a process/journey/escalation: arrival → knock → door opens → face falls). Vertical = emotional alignment (illustrative shots stacked directly on the exact word they reinforce). Rule: place sequential chains horizontally; align illustrative shots vertically, landing the cut on the keyword. Mark every evocative, emotional, or descriptive line as an anchor and ask one question there: "What should I cut to here?"
  • The illustration test. For any shot ask: if I deleted this picture and kept only the audio, would the point still land? Yes → the shot genuinely illustrates; keep it. No → the picture is carrying a logical gap; either the line or the shot is wrong. This is the test that catches "generic clip under a specific line."
  • Three registers — pick one consciously, don't default to literal: Literal narration names a thing → show that thing ("he brought the ring" → the ring). Conceptual narration states an idea → show a concrete stand-in ("the money problems" → a declined card). Metaphorical narration is figurative → a symbolic image whose emotion rings true even with no literal link.
  • Cut on the keyword — break narration into emotional peaks, keywords, and tonal shifts; those are your edit points. Don't cut on a metronome — cut where the meaning turns.
  • Hide every picture change under continuous audio (J-cuts / L-cuts). Let the voice run across the cut (L-cut) so the change of picture feels motivated; bring the next audio in early (J-cut) to pull the viewer into a reveal. A ~2-second overlap is the working default. Don't split-cut every edit — use it where it serves pacing.

2 Making stills + Ken Burns feel dynamic

  • Motivate the move by the content — never a default preset. Plan the zoom/pan path around how the eye should travel through this image. Identical canned moves across every still is the #1 tell of cheap Ken Burns.
  • Direction has meaning — assign it, don't randomize:
    Push IN intimacy, focus, "look closer," a detail/face, tightening tension — reserve for reaction faces and reveals. Pull OUT context, isolation, consequence, "here's the bigger picture" — good for landing a punchline or a twist. Reveal move open on a close detail, then pull out so a second subject enters frame — hand off from one person to the next inside a single image.
  • Vary the axis and the pairing. Alternate horizontal pans, vertical moves, push-ins and pull-outs. Never place two same-direction stills back to back.
  • Always ease. Ease-in/ease-out on the start and end keyframes; a linear zoom that starts and stops abruptly reads as amateur. Match velocity to emotion — slow = contemplative, faster = urgency.
  • Upgrade stills that carry weight to 2.5D parallax. Separate subject from background so they move at different rates, giving real depth. Save it for money moments — the bombshell, the confrontation reveal — not every image.
  • Match-cut between stills. Cut between two stills that share shape, composition, or motion direction so the eye flows straight through. End one still's push-in on a shape that the next still opens on.
  • Hold on reaction. When a beat lands emotionally, a slow push-in on a reaction face held a touch longer than comfortable does more than a fast cut. Save fast cutting for the setup.

3 Avoiding repetitive / stale B-roll

  • Refresh the picture every 3–5 seconds. It takes ~3–5s for a viewer to absorb a shot before attention wavers — make something visually meaningful happen on that cadence.
  • Force a full visual reset every 30–50 seconds. Tighten to every 10–20s in the opening to hook, then widen once the viewer is committed. 45s of unbroken talking-head is a defect regardless of how good the audio is.
  • Coverage thinking — generate the set. For any recurring couple/location, make a wide establisher, a medium two-shot, and tight singles, so you can revisit a scene without repeating the identical frame.
  • Detail / insert shots are the cheapest variety and the most valuable — hands, objects, textures, a phone screen, a ring, a suitcase zipper. Generate an insert for the concrete noun in the line rather than replaying a location loop.
  • Texture / tone shots for the quiet moments — a slow curtain, a clock, a night sky — establish mood and bridge transitions during quieter narration.
  • Don't let one device become the crutch. Rotate devices — Ken Burns still → parallax → hero clip → insert → typography — so no single look dominates a stretch.

4 Visualizing abstract / figurative narration

e.g. "he dropped a grenade on the relationship," "the tension was building"

  • Turn the abstraction into a concrete stand-in. Find the physical object that embodies the idea; close-ups make abstract concepts feel real. "The tension was building" → a kettle, a tightening fist, a fuse.
  • Default move for any figurative line: symbolic insert, ~2s hold, slow push-in. Generate the literal image of the metaphor ("dropped a grenade" → a grenade, a pin being pulled), cut to it on the keyword, hold, then cut back. Literalizing the metaphor is a signature reality-recap comedic beat — lean into it.
  • Juxtapose two shots to manufacture meaning. Cutting between two contrasting/related images lets the viewer draw the connection — that inference is the metaphor (the smiling fiancé → cut to the grenade).
  • Graphic match cut as visual metaphor. Match two images by shape to imply they're the same idea. "Her heart shattered" → match a face to breaking glass.
  • Kinetic typography for the un-image-able and for punchlines. Animate the key word on screen — bold, punchy, on the stressed syllable — for the one or two words that carry the line, not whole sentences.

5 Frameworks worth internalizing

Walter Murch — the Rule of Six

Judge every cut against six priorities, in strict order of importance. Give up the lower one first; never sacrifice the higher.

1 · Emotion51% — the whole ballgame. If the cut doesn't feel right emotionally, nothing else matters.
2 · StoryDoes the cut advance or clarify?
3 · RhythmIs it the right moment?
4 · Eye-traceKeep the point of interest in a consistent screen zone across the cut so the eye isn't thrown.
5–6 · Plane2D screen plane / 3D spatial continuity — lowest priority; sacrifice these before the others.

Practical use: when a technically-clean cut still feels off, it almost always served eye-trace or continuity over emotion — recut for the emotion.

  • Cut for logic (video-essay school). Every cut serves the argument: B-roll illustrates the point, a graphic makes an abstraction concrete, a clip is evidence. Combine with the illustration test.
  • Retention-editing research. The 3–5s change and 30–50s reset numbers above; the "contract renewal" model — your hook buys the first few seconds, and each new visual/rhythm shift renews the lease.
  • Match-cut & creative-transition literature. Graphic vs. thematic vs. audio match cuts as connective and metaphor tools.

6 Build a reusable visual vocabulary

Make these standing tables so you're not re-deciding from scratch each line:

  1. A "verb/emotion → visual move" lookup. reveal/twist = pull-out or J-cut reveal · intimacy/reaction = slow push-in on a face · escalation = sequential chain · figurative line = symbolic insert 2s + push-in · quotable/stat = kinetic type.
  2. A generated-still coverage set per recurring subject. Wide + medium + tight singles + one insert, tagged in the shared library, so revisits vary instead of repeat.
  3. A symbol bank for recurring abstractions. Bombshell, red flag, walking on eggshells, gold-digger, love-bombing — pre-generate the literalized image once and reuse the concept.
  4. A "device rotation" rule of thumb. No single device (Ken Burns still, parallax, hero clip, insert, typography, host loop) more than ~twice in a row; log what you last used.
  5. An anchor-marking pass before building. First pass over the script: mark every keyword / emotional peak / figurative line. Those marks are the shopping list of stills to generate.

Starter checklist — run on every line of narration

  1. Name the register — literal, conceptual, or metaphorical? (Don't default to literal.)
  2. Find the keyword — the cut lands there, not on the sentence boundary.
  3. Generate the specific image for that keyword. Only reach for existing/generic B-roll if the illustration test still passes.
  4. Illustration test — with picture removed, does the point still land? If no, the picture is masking a gap.
  5. Pick a motivated move — push-in (intimacy/detail) vs pull-out (context/twist) vs pan/reveal — different from the previous shot's move. Ease both ends.
  6. Figurative line? → symbolic insert, ~2s hold, slow push-in (or graphic match, or kinetic type on the key word).
  7. Hide the cut under continuous audio (L-cut out / J-cut in, ~2s overlap).
  8. Pacing check — has the picture changed in the last 3–5s? A full reset in the last 30–50s? If not, add one.
  9. Repetition check — same device or subject-frame as the last shot? Rotate.
  10. Murch check — does the cut feel right emotionally first? If a clean cut feels off, recut for emotion over continuity.
