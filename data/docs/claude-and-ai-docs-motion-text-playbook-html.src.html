Motion Text Playbook

How to put words on a video so it looks made by a seasoned content creator, not slapped on. It comes from course ad 03 "Same Face" (solorender). Eddie rejected two plain-caption versions, then called the third "amazing". Everything here is the method that won, with the exact timings used.

Use it for: ads, Shorts, Reels, trailers, explainers, course promos. Any video in any project where words appear on screen.

The five rules

Never break these

1. The text is part of the story, not a caption.First ask: what is this video's one idea, and what graphic shows that idea? "Same Face" was about consistent faces, so the text became a face-recognition scanner, with the match score falling for the random heroes and hitting 100% for Brix. Decide this concept before writing a single line of text. A brief that could fit any other video is generic: rethink it.
2. Never put text on a face.Measure where every face is, frame by frame, with a face detector (below). Then place text only in the empty bands. Never judge it by eye.
3. Nothing appears without moving in.Every line enters with an animation that suits its meaning: a slam for a punchline, a glitch for something broken, a focus-pull for a lesson, a typewriter for tech. Static text appearing and disappearing reads as "basic".
4. Each genre or mood gets its own type.Use one strong main face (Anton) for the voice of the ad. Each scene's title borrows the look of its world: a wanted-poster stamp, art-deco rules, terminal typing, a 70s TV slide.
5. Sound sells the motion.Every slam gets an impact hit, and every hard cut gets a glitch or whoosh. Start an entrance 1–2 frames before its hit so the eye and ear land together.
What got rejected

Eddie's "no" list

  • Plain Bangers captions fading on and off. "no idea what i'm looking at", then "basic as fuck".
  • Text across faces. "Don't put the text on your faces."
  • A cut with no explainer text at all. Viewers need to be told what they're looking at, beat by beat.
  • Generic "fun" music before the concept existed. Three tracks wasted. The winner was music built on the idea: the score changes genre with every cut.
  • Defaulting to the first, easiest treatment. "Basic and bland is your first reaction, get that out of your thinking."
Step one, every time

Map the faces before placing text

The Mac's built-in face detector (Apple Vision) runs free and offline from a tiny Swift script. Pull frames at 5 per second from every shot, detect faces, pad each box to cover hair, hats and chin, and keep text out of it.

// faces.swift — usage: swift faces.swift <folder of PNG frames>
// prints "file x y w h" per face, top-left origin, 0..1
import Foundation; import Vision; import AppKit
let dir = CommandLine.arguments[1]
for f in try FileManager.default.contentsOfDirectory(atPath: dir).filter({ $0.hasSuffix(".png") }).sorted() {
  let url = URL(fileURLWithPath: dir).appendingPathComponent(f)
  guard let img = NSImage(contentsOf: url),
        let cg = img.cgImage(forProposedRect: nil, context: nil, hints: nil) else { continue }
  let req = VNDetectFaceRectanglesRequest()
  try? VNImageRequestHandler(cgImage: cg, options: [:]).perform([req])
  let faces = (req.results ?? []).map { r in String(format: "%.3f %.3f %.3f %.3f",
      r.boundingBox.minX, 1 - r.boundingBox.maxY, r.boundingBox.width, r.boundingBox.height) }
  print(f, faces.isEmpty ? "none" : faces.joined(separator: " | "))
}

# frames:  ffmpeg -i shot.mp4 -vf "fps=5,scale=540:-1" frames/shot_%03d.png
# pad each box: x − 0.28w, y − 0.45h, width ×1.56, height ×1.75 (hair, hat, chin)
# keep the BIGGEST face per frame (ignores crew in the background)

In "Same Face", faces sat between 12% and 53% down the frame. So almost all text went to the band from 58% to 80% down (y 1110–1540 on 1080×1920). Score pills went to whichever side of the face had more room.

Wide 16:9 version

Same pieces, side columns

Widening tall pictures (Bria Expand) leaves the subject in the middle third. In "Same Face" every face sat between 42% and 58% across. So in the wide cut, all words move to two side columns that flank the face. Nothing goes under the chin.

  • Columns are 640 px wide, 70 px in from each edge. The tall components are reused as they are, inside a column scaled with CSS zoom: 0.62.
  • Bug to avoid: zoom also shrinks the column's own left value. Set left: x / zoom, or the right column lands on the face. This happened on the first wide pass.
  • Alternate sides for the genre titles (western left, noir right, sci-fi left, cooking right). Stamp on one side, sentence on the other. Two-part punchlines flank the face ("SAME FACE." left, "EVERY SHOT." right).
  • End card: the frozen frame shrinks to a card on the left (scale 0.52, moved 420 px left), and the course lines sit in the right column.
  • Reference build: src/SameFaceWide.jsx. Final file: Claude outputs/same-face-ad-16x9.mp4.
Genre title cards

Each world gets its own type

Rye · Western

Wanted-poster stamp

Aged-paper plate (radial cream to tan), double brown border, inset burn, tilted −3°. Slams in at 1.9→1 (spring 10/300/0.6) with a small shake and an impact hit. Line underneath: "★ SAME FACE ★". It waits until the action that matters (the doors swinging) has played, so it never covers it.

Limelight · Film noir

Art-deco rules

Two thin white rules and a diamond draw outward from the centre over 15 frames. The title comes in with letter-spacing 30→8 px and blur to sharp. A small spaced subtitle sits below.

Orbitron + Share Tech Mono · Sci-fi

Terminal typing

A cyan panel opens on Y with scanlines and a glow. The title types at 2 frames per letter with a block cursor blinking every 8 frames. Then "> SAME FACE_" types at 1.5 frames per letter.

Shrikhand · 70s TV

Striped-shadow slide

Yellow title with stacked orange / pink / teal offset shadows, tilted −4°. It slides in from off-screen left (spring 10/140) while a pink pill ("same face!") slides the opposite way.

Main voice: Anton for every big line. Small print: Inter 500. All of these are free Google Fonts (OFL), loaded in Remotion with @remotion/google-fonts. Other genre picks from the research: Sancreek (western), Poiret One (deco), Audiowide (sci-fi), Righteous (70s).

How it's built

The pipeline

  1. Concept first. Write the one-line idea, then the graphic that shows it. Only then pick music and text.
  2. Base film without text. Cut the clips, music and sound effects with the libass ffmpeg (Podcast/…/_SHARED/bin/ffmpeg). Use the concat filter, not the concat demuxer. Write the shot start and end times to a JSON file.
  3. Face map. Run faces.swift on 5 fps frames of every shot, and turn the output into timed boxes in the same JSON.
  4. Motion layer in Remotion at studio/tools/explainer. The base film plays as OffthreadVideo, and the text, scanners and cards are React on top. Reference build: src/SameFace.jsx. Copy its components (Scanner, GlitchLine, Slam, Words, LessonEight, the genre cards, EndCard) rather than writing new ones.
  5. Stills first. Before any full render, render about 12 stills at key frames (npx remotion still … --frame=N) and tile them into one sheet. Fix placement, then render a 10-second sample for Eddie, then the whole thing.
  6. Render: npx remotion render src/index.jsx <Comp> out.mp4 --concurrency=8 --browser-executable=~/Library/Caches/ms-playwright/chromium_headless_shell-1243/chrome-headless-shell-mac-x64/chrome-headless-shell. Keep the progress output on.
  7. Finish: two-pass loudnorm to −14 LUFS with true peak −1.5, and convert to yuv420p TV range (Remotion outputs full-range yuvj420p, and effects like rgbashift can produce 4:4:4, which phones may not play).
Before it goes to Eddie

The check

  • A frame grid of the whole video (2–3 frames per second). Zero text on any face, and nothing past the frame edges.
  • Key lines inside x 130–900, y 290–1250.
  • Every line has an entrance and an exit. None just appears.
  • Each slam has its sound hit, landing with the visual.
  • Each line stays up long enough to read: about 0.3 s per word, plus its entrance.
  • Loudness about −14 LUFS, peak under −1 dB, yuv420p, plays on a phone.
  • The words are true. Check names, numbers and claims (lesson titles, plan names) against the real product.
