2. Positioning

The problem with your three audiences

You picked beginners, small business owners, and builders. Those are three channels. They don't share a search vocabulary, a thumbnail style, or a tolerance for jargon. A video that satisfies a Claude Code user will lose a bakery owner in the first 40 seconds, and YouTube's recommendation system will read the mixed signals as an unfocused channel and stop pushing you to anyone.

Pick a spine. The other two become occasional guests.

My recommendation: small business owners and solo professionals

(This section is judgment, not sourced research.)

Against beginners as the spine: the biggest audience and the most brutally saturated. "What is AI," "ChatGPT for beginners," "best AI tools 2026" — thousands of channels, many of them well-funded, all fighting for the lowest-RPM viewers on the platform. You'd be entering the most crowded room with the least differentiated product.

Against builders as the spine: the highest revenue per viewer, and genuinely underserved on Claude Code / MCP / agents. But it contradicts two of your own constraints. Building credible technical content takes more than 5 hrs/week, and the audience is small — incompatible with an ad-revenue-and-scale goal. It's also the audience most likely to run ad blockers, which is precisely the mechanism that craters tech-niche RPM.

For small business owners:

  • Evergreen. "How to stop writing the same customer email forty times a week" is as true in 2028 as today. Model-release content is worthless in six months. At one video a week you cannot afford a decaying library.
  • RPM. Business-education content classifies as education, watches on desktop and TV, and reaches an audience that doesn't block ads. This is where the $10 RPM lives.
  • Buyer intent. Small business owners buy software. That's what makes affiliate revenue work, and affiliate will out-earn AdSense (§7).
  • Less crowded. The AI-education niche is saturated at the tool layer and thin at the job layer. Everyone reviews Claude. Very few show a plumbing contractor how to turn a voicemail into a quote.
  • Beginners come free. SMB owners largely are beginners. You serve both without splitting the channel.

Positioning statement

Practical AI for people who run a business and don't have time to learn AI. One job, one workflow, one video. No hype, no tool tourism, no jargon.

What this rules out

No news roundups. No "top 10 AI tools." No model-release reaction videos. Every one of those is a trap: cheap to make, decays in weeks, competes against channels with full-time staff, and trains YouTube to show you to the wrong audience.

The builder audience isn't wasted

Run it as a secondary pillar at roughly one video in six — Claude Code, MCP, agents. It's high-RPM, it's genuinely underserved, and it gives you somewhere to grow if the SMB angle stalls. Just don't let it drive the channel identity in year one.

3. Brand

Channel name

Hard rule from the research: keep "Claude" and "Anthropic" out of the channel name and @handle. Anthropic's trademark guidelines are permission-only — "You may only use our trademarks as specifically permitted by us and only in materials we approve beforehand" — and their enforcement posture tightened through 2026. A name is the asset most expensive to lose at 50,000 subscribers. Meanwhile YouTube search ranks on title, description, and transcript — not channel name — so putting "Claude" in your titles captures all the SEO value at none of the legal risk. <sub>Source: https://www.anthropic.com/legal/trademark-guidelines</sub>

The character

The single most important design constraint: you have 5 hours a week. Every design decision should be judged by whether it survives contact with that budget.

Design rules:

  1. No mouth, or a mouth that's one shape. Lip-sync is the biggest time sink in character animation and the biggest uncanny-valley risk. Convey emotion through eyes and body tilt. This is the single highest-leverage rule here.
  2. Under six moving parts. Body, two eyes, optional two arms. That's it.
  3. Flat vector, two brand colors plus neutrals. No gradients, no shading, no textures.
  4. Build a pose library once, reuse forever. Draw 12 poses in week one — thinking, pointing, confused, celebrating, tired, presenting left, presenting right, shrugging, alarmed, satisfied, asleep, waving. From week two you are placing poses, not animating.
  5. The character is a pointer, not a performer. It appears at the edges of the frame gesturing at the content. It is not the content. Most of your screen real estate is diagrams and screen recordings.

Why this beats a photoreal avatar (sourced): non-realistic animated content is explicitly exempt from YouTube's altered-or-synthetic-content disclosure requirement, alongside cloning your own voice — both of your choices land in the exempt column. Photoreal avatars would trigger the label, and since May 2026 YouTube auto-detects them via SynthID and C2PA metadata regardless of whether you disclose. <sub>Source: https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491</sub>

Visual system

  • Two brand colors. One warm accent for emphasis, one dark neutral background. Every diagram, thumbnail, and lower-third uses only these. Consistency at 120px is what makes a thumbnail recognizable in a feed.
  • One typeface, two weights. Inter, Poppins, or similar. Free.
  • Thumbnail template: character in a fixed corner + one strong visual metaphor + 3–5 words maximum. Build it once in Canva with swappable layers. Always preview at 120px wide — mobile legibility is the binding constraint, not desktop.
  • A 5-second animated intro, and never longer. Same intro every video is explicitly permitted by YouTube's policy; long intros kill the first-30-second retention window.

4. Content strategy

Three pillars

Why "The Test" pillar is not optional

This is the policy insurance and it's worth understanding properly.

YouTube's inauthentic-content rule is enforced at the channel level — one repeated pattern across 20–30 uploads demonetizes everything, not just the offending videos. In early 2026, 16 channels totalling ~35M subscribers and ~4.7B lifetime views were wiped under it. The prohibited pattern is "AI-generated content made with generic templates giving the impression of mass production without adding the creator's original, authentic insights." <sub>Sources: https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392 · https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-2026/</sub>

The defense is not avoiding AI — YouTube stated plainly that "channels that use AI in their content remain eligible for monetization." The defense is original artifacts: your own test results, your own screen recordings, your own numbers, your own opinion about whether the thing actually worked. A video that says "here's what Claude's new feature does" is templated. A video that says "I ran the same ten quote requests through Claude and ChatGPT, here's where each one broke" cannot be mass-produced by anyone but you.

Vary your structure, not just your topic. Intros and outros can repeat. If your last twenty videos are all hook → three tips → outro with the nouns swapped, that is precisely the pattern under enforcement.

Title formulas that fit this positioning

  • The Job: "Stop [painful task]: The [N]-Minute AI Setup"
  • The Job: "How I [outcome] Without [the thing they dread]"
  • The Test: "I Ran [N] Real [tasks] Through Claude and ChatGPT. Here's What Broke."
  • The Test: "I Let AI Handle [task] for a Week. Here's the Damage."
  • The Build: "Build a [thing] in [time] — No Code"
  • Comparison: "Claude vs ChatGPT for [specific job]" — always the job, never generic

Put "Claude," "ChatGPT," and "AI" in titles freely. That's nominative fair use, it's where the search volume lives, and it's the safe place to use those marks.

5. Production

The weekly workflow — 5 hours, steady state

Script draft + read-aloud rewrite45 minRead it out loud. Cut anything you wouldn't say.
Voice generation + cleanup15 minBatch by section
Diagrams (Napkin / Excalidraw)45 min8–12 per video
Screen recording of the real task30 minThis is your originality artifact. Never skip it.
Assembly, pacing, character placement, music60 minReusable Resolve template
Captions10 minLocal whisper → SRT → styled template

Total~4.8 hrs

Three honest caveats. Weeks 1–4 will run 8–12 hours each while you build the templates — Resolve project, caption style, thumbnail layout, character pose library. That's one-time capital investment, and it's exactly what makes week five fit in five hours. Start at 5–7 minutes, not 10; the longer end doesn't fit the budget until you're months in. And tier ruthlessly: roughly 15% of runtime gets custom animation, 50% gets diagrams with simple motion, 35% is screen recording and kinetic type. Try to custom-animate everything and this becomes a 20-hour job and you'll quit by week six.

Licensing traps — these will bite you

Every one of these is a tool commonly recommended in "best free AI tools" listicles that would put a monetized channel in breach:

  • ElevenLabs free tier — no commercial license, full stop.
  • Edge TTS — the free option everyone recommends. Microsoft's own staff say there's "no public documentation that explicitly addresses commercial usage rights." It's a reverse-engineered internal endpoint. Use Azure F0 or Google Chirp instead.
  • XTTS-v2 / Coqui and Fish Speech — non-commercial licenses.
  • Canva "Popular Music" — personal use only, cannot be monetized. The most common accidental copyright strike among Canva users.
  • Suno free tier — output can never be monetized, even if you upgrade later.
  • FLUX.1 [dev] — non-commercial. FLUX.1 [schnell] is Apache 2.0 and fine. Easy, costly mix-up.
  • Uppbeat free — requires a per-track credit specific to that track and that video. Reusing a track needs a fresh download and fresh credit.
  • CapCut — as of the June 2025 terms, granted a perpetual license over user content including unpublished drafts. Couldn't confirm whether that was walked back. Don't put unreleased work there.

6. Policy safety rules

Nine rules. Follow them and you're in good shape; the enforcement risk here is real but it's specific and avoidable.

  1. Every video contains something only you could have made — your screen recording, your test result, your number, your verdict.
  2. Vary structure across videos. Repeated intro/outro is fine. Repeated body template is the violation.
  3. Clone your own voice. Explicitly exempt from disclosure. A stock TTS voice sits in an unresolved gray area.
  4. Animation stays stylized. Non-realistic is explicitly exempt. Don't drift toward photoreal.
  5. Never post long, uncommented screen captures. YouTube's software policy requires commentary "strictly tied to the live action being shown." Silent or loosely-narrated UI capture is the failure mode.
  6. Prefer your own diagrams over raw UI capture where you can. Reduces reused-content, software-license, and trademark exposure simultaneously.
  7. One video a week, not five a day. Mass cadence is itself a pattern signal.
  8. Avoid AI-persona content on finance, legal, and health. Explicitly non-monetizable as of the July 2026 update. Stay in business-operations territory.
  9. Front-load originality before applying to YPP. Review is manual and holistic. Your first 20–30 videos are your application.

7. Money — realistically

The thresholds (sourced, current as of July 2026)

  • Early access (fan funding, no ads): 500 subs + 3 uploads in 90 days + 3,000 watch hours in 12 months
  • Full YPP (ad revenue): 1,000 subs + 4,000 watch hours in 12 months, or 1,000 subs + 10M Shorts views in 90 days

Shorts watch time does not count toward the 4,000 hours. The Shorts path needs ~110,000 views per day sustained. Ignore it. Target the long-form path. <sub>https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851</sub>

Two things you need to know now

Anthropic pays you nothing. There is no public affiliate or referral program for creators as of July 2026 — only a B2B partner network. Aggregator sites claiming a "20% recurring Claude affiliate program" are fabricating it; one such page's own disclosure reads "initial seed data — verify against official program pages." The tool your channel is most about will not pay you a cent. Build affiliate revenue around adjacent tools.

Verified programs worth building around: Skool (40% of recurring revenue, for life — the best terms in the category, and worth considering as your own community platform), ElevenLabs (22% for 12 months). Notion pays up to $50/signup plus 20% of year-one revenue with a 180-day window but is currently not accepting new affiliates — check back.

On sponsors: there is no subscriber threshold at which brands start reaching out. Views per video and buyer intent matter more than subscriber count. AI tool startups are unusually willing to sponsor small, targeted channels — a 5,000-sub channel of small business owners can outperform a 200,000-sub general channel for the right sponsor. Expect $300–$1,500 per integration in months 6–12. Pitch outbound; don't wait for inbound.

Realistically, your own product — a prompt library, a template pack, a Skool community — will likely exceed all three revenue lines combined by month 18. Keep that in view even though it's out of scope for now.

Shorts

Use them as a distribution channel, never a revenue one. US Shorts RPM is $0.328 per 1,000 views — a long-form view in this niche is worth roughly 15–25x a Shorts view. Repurpose clips from long-form at maybe 20–30% of your effort. Do not build a Shorts-first strategy; it cannot get you to 4,000 hours.

  • [ ] Pick two brand colors and one typeface
  • [ ] Build the reusable assets: Resolve project template, caption style, thumbnail template, 5-second intro
  • [ ] Set up ElevenLabs, clone your voice, test it on a full paragraph
  • [ ] Write channel description including the non-affiliation disclaimer
  • [ ] Validate the video 1–10 topics against real YouTube search (§9)

Weeks 3–6 · Ship four videos

  • [ ] Publish videos 1–4, one per week, same day and time
  • [ ] Expect 8–12 hrs each. It gets faster.
  • [ ] Reply to every single comment. Every one.
  • [ ] Do not check analytics obsessively. Early numbers are noise.

Weeks 7–12 · Find the signal

  • [ ] Publish videos 5–10
  • [ ] After video 8, look at retention graphs, not view counts. Where do people leave? Fix that.
  • [ ] Whichever video outperforms — make two more like it. This is the only reliable growth signal you'll get in year one.
  • [ ] Start cutting one Short per long-form video
  • [ ] Apply to YPP early-access at 500 subs

Realistic expectations: most channels see very little for the first 10–15 videos. The library compounds; individual videos mostly don't. Judge yourself on shipping cadence and retention, not subscribers, until at least video 15.

9. Validate the topic list before you commit

This is the research I didn't finish, and it's worth an hour of your time.

For each of your first ten titles:

  1. Search the exact phrase on YouTube. Filter to This year.
  2. Note how many results are genuinely on topic versus loosely related. Fewer than ~10 on-topic results is a gap worth taking.
  3. Look at the view counts of the top results relative to the channel size. A 5,000-sub channel with a 200,000-view video means the topic is carrying it — that's a topic you want.
  4. Check YouTube's autocomplete: type "claude for" / "ai for small business" / "chatgpt for" and record every suggestion. Those are literal search queries, and they're free keyword data.
  5. Cross-check on Reddit — r/smallbusiness and r/Entrepreneur — for the same question phrased by an actual human. Their phrasing is your title.

Replace any of my thirty titles that fail this test. The list is a starting hypothesis, not a plan.

10. The honest risks

The faceless penalty is real and it got worse in 2026. Since March 2026 YouTube has been testing viewer surveys asking whether a video "feels like AI slop," and adjusting recommendations toward on-camera human hosts. Legitimate voiceover creators — people making that format long before generative AI existed — got caught in the sweep. A stylized character with your real cloned voice and original screen recordings is roughly the safest version of faceless available, but it's a mitigated risk, not an eliminated one.

The mitigation worth considering: add 20–30 seconds of your actual face as a cold open once you're comfortable. It satisfies the human-presenter signal, materially helps sponsorship rates (sponsors discount faceless channels), and costs you nothing but a phone camera. You don't have to be on screen for the other seven minutes.

5 hrs/week is genuinely tight for 6–8 minute animated video. The plan works only if you're disciplined about templates and ruthless about tiering. If week 5 is still taking 12 hours, cut runtime to 5 minutes before you cut cadence — consistency matters more than length.

One video a week means ~30 videos before you know anything. That's seven months. Go in expecting that timeline, or the month-three trough will end the project.
