"The 15-Minute AI Setup That Answers Your Customer Emails"

Runtime target: 6:30 · Pillar: The Job · Audience: Small business owners

Thumbnail

Character on the right, looking exhausted, slumped. Left two-thirds: a towering stack of identical envelope icons. Text, four words, heavy weight: "STOP RETYPING THIS". Accent color on the word STOP only. Check at 120px — if you can't read it, cut a word.

COLD OPEN (0:00–0:22)

[VISUAL: Black. A single email appears, typed out character by character. Then a second, nearly identical. Then a third. They begin stacking and multiplying faster than the eye can follow until the screen is full.]

You've written this email before.

Not one like it. This one. Same question, same answer, same polite closing line you've typed nine hundred times.

[VISUAL: The stack freezes. Character pops in bottom-right, deadpan, staring at the pile.]

If you run a business, somewhere between a fifth and a third of your inbox is a question you have already answered. Today I'm going to show you the exact setup that handles those — start to finish, about fifteen minutes, and you don't need to know anything technical.

[VISUAL: 5-second intro sting]

THE PROBLEM, PROPERLY FRAMED (0:22–1:15)

[VISUAL: Simple diagram — inbox on the left, three labeled buckets on the right.]

Here's the thing most people get wrong when they try this.

They open ChatGPT or Claude, paste in an email, and say "write me a reply." And it does. It writes a perfectly fine reply that sounds like nobody, mentions nothing specific about your business, and takes almost as long to fix as it would have taken to write.

[VISUAL: Character gestures at the three buckets as each is named.]

That's because your inbox isn't one problem. It's three.

Bucket one: questions you've answered before. Hours, pricing, availability, do-you-service-my-area. These are pure repetition, and they're what we're solving today.

Bucket two: things that need a real decision. A complaint, a refund, a negotiation. Don't automate these. I'll come back to why.

Bucket three: everything else — one-offs, weird stuff, actual conversations.

[VISUAL: Bucket one glows in the accent color. Two and three dim.]

We only want bucket one. Trying to solve all three at once is exactly why this fails for most people.

THE SETUP · PART 1 — THE BRIEF (1:15–3:00)

[VISUAL: Character holds up a folder labeled "Your Business."]

The whole trick is this: AI doesn't know your business. So we tell it once, properly, instead of re-explaining in every single prompt.

[SCREEN REC — record this yourself] Open Claude. Create a Project. Name it something like "Customer Replies." Show the Project knowledge panel.

I'm using a Project here — that's just a container that remembers context across conversations. ChatGPT has the same idea, it's called a Custom GPT. Either works.

[SCREEN REC continues — paste in a business brief as you narrate the sections]

Into that container I'm putting one document. Call it the brief. Five sections, and this is the part worth doing carefully, because everything downstream depends on it.

[VISUAL: Five numbered items build on screen as narrated, each with a small icon]

One — what you actually do. Two sentences. Not marketing copy. "We're a two-person plumbing outfit in Portland. Residential only, no commercial."

Two — your facts. Hours. Service area. Price ranges. Lead times. Anything you've answered twice.

Three — your voice. And here's the shortcut that makes this work: don't describe your voice. Paste in five real emails you've actually sent. Nothing you write about your tone will work as well as five examples of it.

Four — your lines in the sand. What it must never do. Never quote a firm price. Never promise a date. Never offer a discount.

Five — the escape hatch. "If the question isn't covered here, don't guess. Say you'll check and get back to them."

[VISUAL: Character taps item five emphatically]

That last one matters more than the other four combined. A confident wrong answer to a customer is worse than no answer at all — and left alone, AI will absolutely give you one.

THE SETUP · PART 2 — THE INSTRUCTION (3:00–4:00)

[SCREEN REC — type the instruction into the Project's custom instructions field]

Now the standing instruction. This goes in the Project settings, and you write it once.

[VISUAL: The text appears clean and readable on screen, large enough to pause and read]

"You draft replies to customer emails for the business described in the brief. Match the tone of the example emails. Only use facts from the brief. If the email asks about something not covered, or contains a complaint, a refund request, or anything involving money changing hands, don't draft a reply — just tell me it needs me. Keep replies under 120 words. Always give me a draft I can edit, never a final send."

[VISUAL: Character points at the last clause]

"Never a final send." Say it in the instruction, and mean it in your process. We're building a drafting assistant, not an autopilot.

IT ACTUALLY WORKING (4:00–5:15)

[SCREEN REC — the payoff. Do this for real, unedited, with three genuine emails.]

Right. Let's run three real ones.

Email one — a straightforward hours-and-availability question. Paste it. Show the draft.

That's sendable. Maybe I change one word.

Email two — something slightly off-brief, e.g. asking about a service you don't offer.

Look at what it did there — it didn't invent an answer, it flagged it. That's the escape hatch working.

Email three — a complaint or refund request. Show it correctly refusing to draft.

And that's the guardrail. It kicked it back to me, which is exactly right, because that email needs a human who can actually make a decision.

[VISUAL: Character nods, satisfied. Three checkmarks appear.]

Three emails, about forty seconds. Two drafts, one correctly refused.

THE HONEST PART (5:15–6:05)

[VISUAL: Character sits down, more serious posture. Background shifts slightly darker.]

Now the part most videos on this leave out.

This will not save you as much time as you think. Reading the draft, checking it, editing it — that's real time. Realistically you're going from four minutes an email to about ninety seconds. On thirty emails a week that's still over an hour back, which is worth having. But anyone promising you a fully automated inbox is selling something.

It will get worse over time if you ignore it. Your prices change, your hours change, you add a service. The brief goes stale and the drafts start being subtly wrong. Put fifteen minutes in your calendar once a month to update it.

Don't connect it directly to your email yet. There are tools that will send automatically. Don't. Run it manually for at least a month first, so you find out what it gets wrong while the stakes are still zero.

[VISUAL: Character holds up bucket two from earlier — the "real decision" bucket]

And bucket two — complaints, refunds, negotiations. Leave those alone permanently. Not because AI can't write them. Because the moment a customer is upset, the thing that fixes it is a person taking responsibility, and a well-worded draft is the opposite of that.

CLOSE (6:05–6:30)

[VISUAL: Character back to neutral. The original email stack from the cold open reappears — then shrinks to a third of its size.]

So: one brief, one instruction, fifteen minutes. Bucket one handled.

If you set this up, the thing I'd actually like to know is what percentage of your inbox turned out to be bucket one. Mine was thirty-eight percent, and that genuinely surprised me. Drop yours in the comments.

[VISUAL: End card — next video thumbnail on the left, subscribe prompt on the right. Character waves.]

Next week I'm running the same ten real business jobs through Claude and ChatGPT side by side to see which one actually holds up. That one's got some results I wasn't expecting.

Production notes for both

Record the screen segments first. They're the constraint — everything else assembles around them. If a demo doesn't work on camera, you need to know that before you've written six minutes of voiceover around it.

Video 2 must be recorded before it's written. Don't script the findings and then run a test that confirms them. Run it, see what happens, then write. If the result is "they're basically the same at most things," that's a fine and honest video — and it will outperform a fake dramatic one, because viewers can tell.

Character usage: roughly 8–12 appearances per video, 2–4 seconds each. Enough for brand recall, not so much that you're animating for hours. The character punctuates; the diagrams and screen recordings carry the information.

Pace: a visual change every 8–10 seconds. Nothing static on screen for more than about twelve. Both scripts are built to that rhythm — if you rewrite sections, preserve it.

The two retention danger zones are the first 30 seconds and the 25–35% mark. Both cold opens are built to survive the first. For the second, note that both scripts put something genuinely useful at roughly the two-minute mark — the brief structure in video 1, the actual test results in video 2. Don't reorder that.
