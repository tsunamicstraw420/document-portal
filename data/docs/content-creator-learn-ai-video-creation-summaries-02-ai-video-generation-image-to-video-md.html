AI Video Generation | Image to Video

Module: AI Video Creation · Instructor: Anthony · Length: \~22 min Lesson URL: https://www.contentcreator.com/products/ai-creator-course/categories/2157469701/posts/2186925504

Summary

A deep dive into the image-to-video workflow, which Anthony calls one of the most powerful and his most-recommended method for cinematic AI content. He opens with a showcase — a cinematic "baby dinosaur's life cycle" short film — made entirely with image-to-video in about an hour for very little money (something that previously would have taken weeks and thousands of dollars).

Core concept

  • Image-to-video: take a static image → upload to an image-to-video tool → write a prompt describing what should happen → animate it. Repeat with different images and edit the clips together.
  • vs. Text-to-video: with text-to-video you start from nothing but a prompt, so much more is left to chance and prompts must be extremely detailed (his "cat jumping on a chair" example — what breed? indoors/outdoors? day/night? weather?). Image-to-video is cheaper and faster because you refine an inexpensive image first, then animate it with a simple prompt. The image already supplies subject, style, environment, lighting, and time of day. Ideal for putting yourself in a video — upload a real photo to Nano Banana Pro as reference rather than describing your appearance in words.

Tools \& access

Favorites are Kling ("Cling") and Veo. Both produce great results; he uses Kling most because it's cheaper and only switches to the pricier Veo when a shot isn't working. Access either via direct paid subscriptions or through a credit-based marketplace like promptedit.com (no subscription — buy credits usable across all tools). The same tool gives the same quality regardless of platform.

PromptEdit demo (image-to-video)

Generative AI → Video → choose tool (he uses Kling 2.6; versions like 2.1, 3.0 also exist — newest is usually best, older saves credits). Upload a start frame, write a simple prompt ("The dinosaur looks at the lightning and then runs away"), set aspect ratio (16:9), set duration (5 vs 10 sec — he uses 5 almost always, since the average Hollywood shot is under 5 seconds and 10s wastes credits), optionally toggle Generate Sound (SFX/dialogue), then Generate. The right panel shows the model, prompt (click to copy), parameters, and reference image. A History panel lets you Reuse a past generation to reload its image + prompt.

Prompt cheat sheet (downloadable under the video)

The image covers character, style, environment, weather, and lighting. Your prompt must add three things:

  1. Subject action (most important) — exactly what the subject does.
  2. Camera movement — if you don't define it, the AI does anything. He prefers clean static shots ("no camera movement" / "tripod shot"). Core moves mirror Hollywood: pan left/right, tilt up/down, tracking, push in/out, jib up/down. Keep it simple for a more professional look.
  3. Emotion / mood / tone — the "why and how" (running scared vs. jogging happily; tense vs. motivational).

Full project walkthrough (the dinosaur video)

  1. Idea — brainstormed with ChatGPT (Gemini/Claude also fine); used its ideas to write his own script, then had ChatGPT give feedback to tighten it (didn't copy verbatim).
  2. Voiceover — generated with ElevenLabs (via PromptEdit).
  3. Character template — built in Nano Banana Pro as a single "source of truth" for a consistent character across every shot; had ChatGPT write the detailed Nano Banana prompt.
  4. Per-scene images — copied each script line into ChatGPT to get a tailored image prompt, then generated in Nano Banana Pro with the character template uploaded as reference (2K, 2 generations to compare). Pro tip: keep image and video tabs open side by side to work faster.
  5. Animate \& edit — animated each image in Kling, downloaded favorites, and edited in any software (shows Premiere Pro; just drag clips to the timeline, set order, trim timing). No advanced editor required.

Pro tips

  • Coverage: show a subject from multiple angles for depth (T-Rex foot hovering → stomp, made by prompting Nano Banana "stomp," then a wide shot, then a menacing eye close-up). Cutting between angles makes edits feel immersive.
  • Sound effects: add them via Kling/Veo or PromptEdit's sound-effect generator.
  • Even for long-form talking-head content, use image-to-video to create B-roll/cutaways (e.g., an AI shot of yourself atop Everest for an entrepreneurship point) so viewers aren't stuck watching a desk the whole time. Long-form clone workflows are covered in other lessons.

Template prompt elements

Subject Action

→ What is the subject doing?

“The woman is turning slowly to face the camera,”

“A bird is soaring across a mountain ridge,”

“A samurai stands still as cherry blossoms fall.”

Camera Movement

→ What’s the motion of the camera?

“A slow dolly-in from behind,”

“360 orbit around the subject,”

“Cinematic handheld zoom out.”

Emotion / Mood / Tone

→ What should the viewer feel?

“Evokes loneliness and wonder,”

“Feels tense and dramatic,”

“Hopeful and inspiring.”

A lone astronaut drifts weightlessly through the void, silhouetted against the curve of Earth, their tether slowly unraveling behind them.

The camera performs a slow, upward dolly-in from below, stars glimmering with subtle parallax as the vastness of space surrounds them.

Evokes a deep sense of isolation, wonder, and the fragility of life.

A barefoot child darts through a sea of golden sunflowers, arms stretched out like airplane wings, laughter echoing faintly in the breeze.

The camera gently tracks from the side at golden hour, lens flaring with every turn toward the setting sun.

Feels warm, joyful, and soaked in nostalgia.
