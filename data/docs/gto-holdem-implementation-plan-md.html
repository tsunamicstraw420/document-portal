GTO Holdem — Implementation Plan

Written 2026-10-06 from the deep-research run plus three follow-up research checks (licensing, tournament math, library cost). Updated the same day with the file layout, GitHub, Cloudflare R2, the fast-track schedule, the AWS account and the table sizes. Every number carries a label telling you how far to trust it:

  • [Verified]: passed a 3-checker adversarial fact-check against the source.
  • [Checked]: one research agent read the primary source (official docs, repo, or paper) and quoted it.
  • [Estimate]: our own arithmetic or judgment, built on the numbers above. Treat it as a planning guess.

1. The plan on one page

What we're building: your own NLHE study and drill system, covering preflop charts, postflop solutions, push/fold, ICM, and a trainer. It's for all four formats you play: 6-max cash, MTTs, Spin & Go, and live full-ring. It starts as your personal tool and is designed so it can become a paid web app later.

The six ideas the whole plan rests on:

  1. The library is the product. The solver is the machine that produces it. Drills need instant answers, but a solve takes minutes, so the trainer serves answers that were solved ahead of time and stored. That's how GTO Wizard works too: its stock libraries are pre-computed with standard solvers to ≤0.3% of pot, and rivers are re-solved live [Checked].
  2. There's a license firewall. The best free solvers are AGPL, which is "share-alike". We run them only offline, on machines you control, and the website serves only their output files. Output of an AGPL program isn't covered by the AGPL (AGPL §2, FSF FAQ) [Checked]. The paid app itself uses only permissively licensed code (MIT, Apache, BSD) or our own code.
  3. The postflop engine is b-inary's postflop-solver, not TexasSolver.
  • postflop-solver was about 5× faster in its author's benchmark: 12–15 s versus 67–96 s, using a quarter to half the RAM (0.68–1.27 GB vs 2.84 GB) [Checked].
  • TexasSolver has a known accuracy bug: it slightly misweights turn and river cards. The bug has been open since 2022, and a fix was only proposed on 2026-10-06 [Checked].
  • TexasSolver stays on as a second-opinion cross-check.
  1. Start with short stacks, not deep cash. Push/fold and ICM are exact, cheap and checkable against published tables, and they serve three of your four formats. Deep-stack preflop charts need the hardest piece in the plan, a full-game preflop solver, so that comes later.
  • Short stacks also become the first paid product (§4, Launch 1).
  • This reverses what I told you earlier, that cash would come first. The research showed deep-stack cash depends on the hardest component.
  1. Spins are mostly chip-EV. On GGPoker's Spin & Gold 3-max table, 97.6% of games are winner-take-all. In those games ICM equals chip share, so cash-style "chip-EV" solutions are exactly right. ICM only matters on the ×10 wheel and above [Checked].
  2. Rent compute, don't buy it. Every spot is an independent job. A rented 32-core machine costs about $0.60/hour on AWS spot pricing [Checked], so a solid cash library comes to roughly $600 [Estimate]. Your Intel MacBook is for building and testing, not for weeks of nonstop solving.

Timeline, fast track [Estimate]: this assumes daily working sessions, with me building and you reviewing, and independent pieces built side by side.

  • First usable trainer (push/fold + ICM): about 2–3 weeks.
  • Personal postflop study on your Mac: about 1–1.5 months.
  • First paid product, a short-stack trainer for push/fold, ICM and Spin & Go: about 2–3 months.
  • GTO-Wizard-style cash drills from your own library: about 3–4 months.
  • Full paid product (all formats, own engine): about 5–6 months.

If we work only a few times a week, roughly double each of these.

First step: Phase 0 (section 4).

3.3 Technology choices

  • Rust for all the number-crunching.
  • The free poker engines worth borrowing from are already Rust: postflop-solver, robopoker, poker-cfr and rs-poker.
  • Rust can also compile to WebAssembly, so later our own solver could run inside a customer's browser at zero server cost.
  • Rust isn't installed on your Mac yet [Checked: cargo/rustc not found].
  • TypeScript + React for the web app. Node 26 is already installed [Checked].
  • Version 1 is a local web page that reads the library files and needs no server.
  • Accounts, payments and hosting come only in Phase 8.
  • Library format:
  • Store flop strategies only: about 0.1–0.3 MB per solved flop after compression [Estimate].
  • A full saved tree including turn and river is gigabytes [Checked: Pio docs; postflop-solver issue #31 shows a 1.8 GB save].
  • Turn and river come later through on-demand re-solving (Phase 7).
  • The library's master copy lives in Cloudflare R2, which is cloud file storage. Rented solving machines upload straight to it, and later the paid app serves files from it.

Done when:

Done when:

  2. Push/fold matches HRC's published heads-up Nash tables (1–200bb in 0.05bb steps) [Checked] at 20+ sampled depths. Only borderline mixed hands may differ.

Phase 4: Preflop solver for deep stacks (XL, the biggest technical risk)

You get: your own preflop charts for:

  • cash at 6-max, 7-handed and 9-handed, at 100bb;
  • MTTs at 7-, 8- and 9-handed, at the MTT stack depths with big-blind ante;
  • Spin, 3-handed at 25bb and below.

Every postflop library needs these as its starting ranges.

  • How paid tools did it:
  • GTO Wizard's original cash preflop came from MonkerSolver on a very large server [Checked].
  • PeakGTO's preflop comes from HRC or MonkerSolver [Checked].
  • Our approach is the same family as Monker and Pluribus:
  • Monte Carlo CFR over the whole hand. It samples the game instead of walking every branch. [Verified] that it's the right tool for whole-game solving.
  • Hand bucketing on later streets, which groups similar hands. MonkerSolver's guide recommends 15–30 buckets per street [Checked; vendor guide].
  • A small bet menu.
  • Table sizes (decided 2026-10-06):
  • Cash: 6-max, 7-handed and 9-handed.
  • MTTs: 7-, 8- and 9-handed.
  • Each table size is its own preflop solve.
  • The postflop solver doesn't change, because almost every postflop pot is heads-up whatever the table size. Table size only changes the preflop ranges that go into each pot.
  • Build order:
  1. Heads-up 100bb first. It's two players, so we can measure exploitability properly.
  2. Then 6-max 100bb cash.
  3. Then MTTs at 8-max, the first MTT table size, at the MTT stack depths.
  4. Then Spin, 3-handed at 25bb.
  5. Next step, once 6-max is proven: 7- and 9-handed cash, then 7- and 9-handed MTTs. This step is approved separately (see §5).
  • Test to run here: can late seats be shared?
  • The cutoff, button and blinds have the same number of players left to act at any table size, so their ranges should be close [Estimate].
  • If the 6-max and 9-handed solves confirm that within our accuracy target, those postflop spots get reused across table sizes. 7- and 9-handed then only add the early-seat spots, which lowers their library cost.
  • Seat matchups grow quickly with table size. Counting single-raised pots only, there are 15 at 6-max, 21 at 7-handed and 36 at 9-handed.
  • Compute:
  • Rented big-memory servers for days.
  • Reference: the Pluribus 6-max blueprint took 12,400 core-hours and under 512 GB of RAM [Verified]. At today's about $0.019 per core-hour that's about $235 per run, and expect several runs while tuning [Estimate].
  • Big-memory machines cost more than the compute machines priced in §5. That price hasn't been researched yet.
  • Your part: create an AWS account with a payment method before this phase. It's the first time we rent machines, and only you can create the account.
  • Caveat: with 3+ players there is no guarantee of "unexploitable" [Verified: Pluribus paper]. Multi-way charts are validated by stability and sanity checks, not by a single exploitability number.

Done when:

  1. The heads-up solution's measured exploitability falls steadily as iterations increase.
  2. 6-max charts are stable across two independent runs, and they pass sanity rules: ranges widen from UTG to BTN, and the blinds defend sensibly.
  3. You spot-check them by eye against charts you trust. You compare only; nothing is imported.

We build these charts ourselves. Licensing outside preflop charts is ruled out (your decision, 2026-10-06).

If quality stalls: spend more compute, use finer hand buckets, and carry over the settings that tested well heads-up. Meanwhile, personal study keeps using hand-entered ranges.

  • re-drills your mistakes more often (spaced repetition);

Phase 7: Our own postflop engine (XL; needed for the full launch, not for Launch 1)

Why: storing turn and river data for a whole library runs into terabytes [Estimate]. So paid tools re-solve later streets on demand, and GTO Wizard re-solves rivers live [Checked]. 11. Algorithm cheat sheet (plain English)

  • CFR (counterfactual regret minimization): the solver plays the spot against itself millions of times. Each time it shifts toward the actions it "regrets" not taking. The average strategy converges to equilibrium.
  • CFR+: a faster variant that never lets regrets go negative. Typically 10× or more faster than plain CFR [Verified].
  • DCFR (Discounted CFR): discounts early, bad iterations. It matched or beat CFR+ everywhere tested, usually reaching 2–3× lower exploitability [Verified]. This is the right algorithm for single postflop spots. postflop-solver uses it, with its own parameter tweaks [Verified].
  • MCCFR (Monte Carlo CFR): samples parts of the game instead of walking every branch. It's needed for whole-game and preflop solves. Pluribus used it [Verified].
  • Abstraction / bucketing: NLHE has about 10^165 game states [Verified], so whole-game solvers group similar hands into buckets and limit bet sizes. Single-spot postflop solvers don't need hand buckets.
  • Bet-size mapping: when a user picks a size that isn't in the tree, map it to the nearest solved sizes with the pseudoharmonic mapping Pluribus used [Verified].
  • Exploitability: how much a perfect opponent could win against the strategy.
  • Full games measure it in mbb/hand, where about 1 means essentially solved [Verified].
  • Single spots measure it as % of pot: 0.3–0.5% is the practical target, and GTO Wizard's libraries are ≤0.3% [Checked].
  • ICM (Malmuth-Harville): your chance of finishing 1st equals your share of the chips. Then the same rule is applied to who's left for 2nd, and so on. Multiply each finish by its prize and add them up.
  • It's slightly biased against reality: off by an average of $0.37 per $100 pool in one study [Checked].
  • FGS: looks a hand or two ahead instead of "paying out now". It matters only short-handed with under about 10bb [Checked].
  • Bubble factor: $ lost when you lose ÷ $ gained when you win. Required equity = BF ÷ (BF + 1) [Checked].
