What the video teaches

Five parts, in his order

Part 1 · Seedance 2.5 manual · 0:00–23:37

The core idea: each kind of input has one job. Words say what happens and what the camera does. Pictures carry faces, clothes, places and look. A video carries motion. A music track carries timing. If a detail can live in a reference, keep it out of the words. His prompt rules: link actions with "while" and "then"; give each person one action per clip; name concrete things and materials, not adjectives; use real camera terms (dolly, orbit, crane), at most three moves, most important first, each tied to a subject. For picture-to-video: leave empty space where the movement goes; keep the face big enough in frame or it drifts; describe only the change. He also covers: draft at low resolution; copy motion from a video of yourself or a dancer; restyle your own phone footage; give every reference exactly one job, because too many references blur faces together; chain long scenes from the last frame and repeat the lighting line word for word; name the sounds you want and write the spoken line out. He ends by running one prompt on four models to show each one's "default taste".

Part 2 · Gemini Omni review · 23:37–43:00

Physics tests: plan for two or three takes. A five-picture character pack works only if it covers the angles the clip needs. With front-only photos, the model quietly avoids turning the head. Editing by conversation: change one thing at a time ("add a plate, keep everything else the same"). Changing the framing breaks it, so treat a reframe as a new scene. He also edits real footage: a glass swapped for a mug, the background swapped for a café. Then a pipeline: make the hero picture first, then animate it. He closes with a head-to-head against Seedance 2.0 and a list of Omni's limits.

Part 3 · Seedance 2.0 test builds · 43:00–57:56

"Timeline prompting" as eight blocks: subject, wardrobe, setting, light, mood, music, colour, what happens second by second, plus a film reference. He uses his site's "enhance prompt" button to write those blocks from one sentence. Reference rules: five pictures is his sweet spot, more starts blending faces. He drafts at 720p, then makes the final at full size. Four builds: a software ad (the woman swipes a laptop like a phone, so tell the model where hands go), a fight scene, a talking-head clip with the line written into the prompt, and a product ad with leather gloves. The pull-on motion failed three times, so his advice is to design the ad around what the model can do. He finishes with a four-model comparison.

Part 4 · Seedance 4K on Higgsfield · 57:56–68:38

Make the perfect still first and pick the best of several. Then animate it with a motion-only prompt written in film language: shot type, lens feel, light direction, mood, the exact movement. He shows three looks with the same method (animation, fantasy, realism) and a 720p vs 1080p vs 4K test on a fast, detailed shot. Last, he plugs the site into Claude as a connector and asks Claude to turn a product page into an ad.

Part 5 · Long videos in Grok · 68:38–83:47

An AI chat writes a 30-second, two-character story, then splits it into 6-second scene prompts. He makes a start picture, then a clip, then presses "extend" over and over. When the chain hits its limit, he saves the last frame and starts a new chain from it. Then he upscales, assembles in CapCut and exports. For multi-minute videos: plan chapters, keep one master character description pasted into every prompt, regenerate a bad scene before extending it, name the time of day in every prompt, and do a 30-second test first.

Detailed examples

His steps, rewritten for solorender

Each example below is draft lesson text in our course's voice, using our running example: @agnes, 84, secretly a speed demon, racing the town's downhill soapbox derby. The method is his. The words, story and prompts are ours. Every button named is one I found in our code today.

Example 1 · Lesson 11 upgrade · "Direct the clip, don't describe it"

A clip prompt has one job: say what happens, in order, and what the camera does. Agnes's face, her biker vest and her cart are already in the start picture. Words about them only compete with it.

WeakAgnes, an 84-year-old woman in a leather vest, races down the hill in her wooden cart, smiling and waving, overtaking everyone, fast, exciting, epic, cinematic.
StrongAgnes tucks low over the steering rope while the red cart behind her clips a hay bale and spins out. Then the crowd on the pavement throws their hats in the air. Camera: low tracking shot beside her front wheel, then a slow rise that shows the street dropping away below. Sound: wooden wheels rattling over wet cobbles, a crowd roaring.
  • One action per person. Agnes tucks. The red cart spins out. The crowd throws hats. If Agnes also needs to wave, that's the next clip.
  • Link them with "while" and "then" so the model knows the order.
  • Name things, not feelings. "Wet cobbles" and "hay bale" come back the same every take. "Exciting" doesn't.
  • At most two or three camera moves, the important one first, each tied to something: "beside her front wheel", not "move right".
  • Then press ✨ Direct · free if you want it polished. It keeps your action and fills the Avoid box.

Example 2 · Bonus lesson 23 · "Act it out yourself" (his reference-video step)

Some motion is easier to perform than to describe. You can't write how Agnes leans into a corner, but you can do it in a kitchen chair.

  1. Prop your phone on a shelf so it doesn't move. Sit facing it with your whole upper body in frame. Grip an imaginary rope and lean hard left, then right, then duck. Keep it under 30 seconds. 8 is plenty.
  2. In solorender, open Studio → Clip tools → DreamActor Act It Out.
  3. In Character, pick the @agnes picture whose shape you want. The finished clip takes that picture's shape, so use a tall picture for a tall clip.
  4. In Your acting, upload your phone clip. There's no prompt to write. The picture decides the look and your clip decides the motion.
  5. Press Generate. An 8-second clip is 100 credits. Your own recording's sound is kept on the result.
  6. If you spoke, her mouth moves when you talk but won't match exactly. For true lip sync, run Lip Sync on the finished clip (lesson 13).

Rules from the video that fit ours: a steady camera (a moving camera fights the motion), the whole body in frame (anything cut off gets invented), and face the camera. A turn away from the lens has nothing to copy a face from.

Example 3 · Bonus lesson 25 · "A three-minute film" (his Grok extend loop)

  1. Ask Claude for the story, then: "Split it into numbered scenes of about 6 seconds. Each scene says only what is seen: who moves, where, the camera, the light. Same two characters, same street, no new people."
  2. Keep one master line for the light and time, for example "grey late-afternoon light, wet cobbles, autumn". Paste it word for word at the end of every scene prompt. The model forgets otherwise.
  3. Make scene 1 in Studio → Video lane from @agnes's start picture. Grok Video 1.5 Lite is the budget pick (40 credits per 5 s).
  4. Open Clip tools → Grok Imagine Extend, choose the clip, paste scene 2, and add 6 seconds (120 credits). Repeat until the clip reaches 15 seconds. Extend only takes clips up to 15 s.
  5. At 15 s, open the clip and press 📸 Last frame, then Animate. That still becomes the start of the next chain, so faces carry over.
  6. If a scene comes back wrong (the vest changed colour, a stranger walked in), don't extend it. Make it again first. Every later scene inherits the mistake.
  7. Optional: Topaz Video Upscale each finished chain.
  8. Open the Video editor, add every chain from your Library in order, add music or sound effects in the sound panel, fade in at the start and out at the end, then export.

Do a 30-second version first. For a film of several minutes, plan it in chapters (opening, trouble, race, finish), and give Claude one chapter at a time. One thing I haven't checked yet: whether Extend returns the whole longer clip or only the new seconds. I'll check it on the first example run before the lesson says either.

Example 4 · Bonus lesson 24 · "A product ad that keeps the product"

Our own product, not his gloves: Agnes's brass pocket stopwatch, the one the derby is timed on.

  1. Make three pictures of the stopwatch: front, back, and a close-up of the engraving. Use Nano Banana 2 Edit from one approved picture, so they match.
  2. Save all three as one Prop entity: @stopwatch.
  3. In Studio → Video lane → Kling O3 · Entities, write: "An old hand opens @stopwatch on a sunlit wooden bench and the second hand starts to sweep. Slow push in to the engraving. Sound: a soft click, a ticking."
  4. Check the details every take: does the engraving stay the same, and does the brass stay brass?
  5. Design around what the model does well. Opening a lid or turning the watch in light works. Fiddly hand actions (winding the crown, fastening a strap) bend things the wrong way. Show the product still, and move the camera instead.

Example 5 · Bonus lesson 22 · "Every model has a taste"

Give four models the same picture and the same short prompt, and you get four different films. Each one fills the gaps in your prompt with its own habits. So pick the model whose habits match your film.

  1. Take @agnes's start picture at the top of the hill. Write one plain prompt: "Agnes pushes off and the cart rolls down the hill. The crowd cheers."
  2. Run it four times in Studio → Video lane: Seedance 2.5, Kling v3 Pro, Veo 3.1 Lite, Grok Video 1.5 Lite.
  3. Watch them side by side and write one line per model: who cuts between angles, who holds one shot, who looks like film, who looks like a phone.
  4. That table becomes your "which model for which film" sheet. The lesson shows ours, from our real runs, not his.

Example 6 · Bonus lesson 25 · "Let Claude make it" (his connector step)

  1. In solorender, open Connect Claude from the sidebar and press Create key. Copy the URL straight away. It's shown once.
  2. In Claude, open Settings → Connectors → Add custom connector. Name it solorender, paste the URL and choose No sign-in. The key inside the URL is the sign-in.
  3. Test it by asking Claude: "check my solorender balance".
  4. Then ask for real work: "Make a 15-second ad for the stopwatch on Kling O3 · Entities using @stopwatch, then put it in my derby project." Claude runs it on your credits, and the result lands in your Library.

Treat the URL like a password. Anyone with it can spend your credits. Revoke it on the same page if it leaks.
