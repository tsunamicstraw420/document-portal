Can Solorender get its AI costs down to rival prices?

Your investor letter (section 9) admits some rivals sell below what suppliers charge Solorender. This page checks which rivals, on which models, how they likely do it, and the steps that would cut Solorender's own costs. It covers what each step costs up front, what it saves, and when it opens up. Nothing was bought, switched or repriced. Every price was read live today unless marked.

  1. No outside customer has run a model yet. All 1,804 creations in the database are from your account or a test account. So "top-selling" here means your own most-used models, and no supplier volume discount is open to us on usage today.
  2. Rivals mostly don't buy cheaper. They sell at about the supplier's list price on monthly plans, then far below it on yearly plans. That only works if customers leave credits unused (every rival's credits reset or vanish), if investor money covers the loss, or if they have a private deal. Each of these has evidence (section 2). Only the arithmetic is certain.
  3. The best step costs nothing: Google sells Nano Banana Pro at $0.067 instead of $0.134, and Nano Banana 2 at $0.034 instead of $0.067, if the customer can wait (Google aims at under 24 hours). That would take Nano Banana Pro from 35 to 17 credits.
  4. The Runware deposit is real, but it pays back only with sales. $25,000 buys $35,000 of credit (30% off). In October we spent about $20 at Runware. The credit never expires, so the $10,000 bonus isn't lost, but the cash sits idle until customers arrive.
  5. Cost cuts alone can't reach rival yearly prices while Solorender keeps the 2.5× rule and credits that never expire. Matching them means changing the price rule or the credit rule, and both are your call. I recommend neither yet (section 4).

2How they get there

Kling, Veo, Nano Banana, Seedance, Grok and ElevenLabs are closed models: only the maker or its licensed resellers can run them. So a rival can't get below our cost on them by running its own computers. Only five things can explain it. Each is marked by how strong its evidence is:

Proven a page says it outright · One source one page says it · Guess it fits the facts, but no page says it for that rival

The arithmetic that is certain

Sunra says on its own site that it sells "every model at its maker's API price", and its list prices match what we pay (Nano Banana Pro 14 credits = $0.14; Google charges $0.134). Its biggest yearly plan then sells each credit for $0.00366, about 37¢ on the dollar. OpenArt's monthly Starter plan sells Kling at $0.35, exactly fal's list price, and its yearly Wonder plan at $0.165, about 47¢ on the dollar.

So on their cheapest plans these rivals charge about a third to a half of the supplier's list price. If customers used every credit, they would lose money on every job unless they buy far below list. Every one of them makes plan credits disappear: Sunra "do not roll over", OpenArt "do not carry over" and are lost on cancel, Higgsfield "don't roll over", Artlist "set to zero" when the plan ends, Krea and VEED "do not roll over". Solorender's credits never expire, so this cushion isn't open to us.

Credits customers never use

Proven policy

The rules are proven on each rival's own page (quoted above). How much goes unused is unknown. No AI studio publishes it, and the only general figures the research found came from vendor blogs about gift cards (a few percent up to 15%), which aren't data about AI studios.

  • Magnific: yearly credits "valid for 1 year"; bought top-up credits are "forfeited" if you cancel.
  • Higgsfield and Krea: bought packs expire after 90 days.

"Unlimited" that slows down

Proven

Magnific sells Kling 2.5 and Nano Banana 2 as "unlimited". Its own help text says that after a hidden priority allowance "generations run one at a time, and speed varies". It is moving Nano Banana Pro, GPT Image and Flux.2 Max out of Unlimited at each customer's next renewal. Higgsfield: unlimited runs "in the standard queue", "personal, human use" only, capped at 720p and 8 s for Seedance. Higgsfield's own pricing page also says unlimited is "subject to dynamic speed adjustments".

Pulling its most expensive model out of Unlimited suggests unlimited Nano Banana Pro cost Magnific too much. That last step is my reading, not their words.

Investor money

Proven for Higgsfield
  • Higgsfield raised $400M at a $5.4B value in August 2026 (TNW, BNN Bloomberg, Pulse 2.0) and claims a $700M yearly revenue pace. No outlet reports a profit.
  • A Chinese tech outlet (36Kr) says Higgsfield sells model calls "at near-cost" and profits elsewhere. One source
  • OpenArt $30M (January 2026) and Krea $47M were found only in search snippets. Not confirmed
  • Sunra: no funding found. Its yearly prices at 37¢ on the dollar have no visible means of support beyond unused credits. Guess

Volume deals with the makers

Guess for any rival

The discounts exist. Google's price page offers "Volume-based discounts (based on usage)" on its Enterprise tier. fal's docs say enterprise customers "can receive custom per-endpoint pricing and volume discounts". Runware says high-usage customers "can contact sales for custom pricing". None publish numbers. No page shows a rival's contract terms. Fortune says Magnific (formerly Freepik) offers Veo 3.1 and Seedance 2.0, about $230M a year in sales, and "profitable" (the founder's own claim) with no venture money. A profitable company selling Nano Banana Pro at $0.05 suggests it pays Google less than list. That is an inference.

A Higgsfield–ByteDance (BytePlus) partnership for Seedance appears only in secondary write-ups, with no announcement found. Not confirmed

Yearly cash up front

Proven

Every rival pushes yearly plans: Krea "Save 40%", Magnific "Save 25%", Higgsfield up to 30%, OpenArt about 30% (its page says "up to 27%"). The cash arrives before the work does, which pays for deposits like Runware's. It doesn't lower the cost of a job by itself.

Running their own computers

Can't explain closed models

Freepik/Magnific runs open FLUX models on up to 500 rented GPUs, according to a case study by its GPU provider Verda. That helps only on open models. On Kling, Veo, Nano Banana or Seedance it is impossible without a licence deal, and none was found.

Most likely explanation, in order: (1) unused credits plus throttled "unlimited", which every rival has; (2) investor money, proven for Higgsfield; (3) private volume deals, likely for the biggest (Magnific, Higgsfield) but not shown anywhere; (4) yearly cash, which helps but can't explain it alone; (5) own computers, which can't apply to these models.

Deeper dig (30 October, your comment): how do Sunra and OpenArt make money at the maker's price?

In short: neither company publishes its margins, so no page proves how they make money. What the pages do show: both sell only a little below the maker's price unless you buy a yearly or big plan, and those cheap credits vanish every month if you don't use them. For pictures there is a real, published half-price route (Google's Flex and Batch tiers, which Solorender now uses for Saver). For video there isn't one: the deepest published discount from Kling, ByteDance, xAI or MiniMax is 10–20% off. So anyone selling video at half the maker's price is either on a private deal, spending investor or startup credits, or losing money on heavy users and making it back on light users' expired credits.

Sunra

  • Who it is: a small company, Second Enlightenment Global Private Limited, registered in Singapore (its own terms page). LinkedIn lists it as London-based, founded 2025, 2–10 staff. No funding, revenue or user numbers found anywhere. one source for size
  • A 5% fee on its developer API. The API page says Sunra charges the maker's price "with no fees on top". Its terms (6.2) add "a service fee, currently 5%" to every top-up, shown before you pay and non-refundable. I read both pages myself. proven
  • Pay-as-you-go credits sit at the maker's price. A credit costs $0.01, so its Nano Banana Pro is $0.14 against Google's $0.134. The monthly Basic plan is also $0.01 a credit. Only yearly and bigger plans go well below: Creator yearly works out to $0.0037. proven
  • Plan credits die monthly, even on yearly plans: "Credits not used before they expire are lost" (terms 5.2). proven
  • It offers big buyers "up to 40% off models' API prices" (API page). So it can sell, or is willing to sell, well below list. The page doesn't say how. proven claim, guess as to how
  • No partner deals found: no "official partner" badge, launch-partner claim or maker deal. Logos of Google, ByteDance and others appear with no stated relationship.

OpenArt

  • Funding and size: $30M Series A in January 2026, led by Canaan with Basis Set and DCM. At that point it had "8M MAU and $70M+ ARR", grown 7× in 2025 with about 20 people (Canaan's own post, which I read; OpenArt's blog gives the same figures). proven
  • Now claims "close to $200M ARR" in a paid press release (21–22 October 2026), still with "more than 8 million" monthly users. A research firm (Sacra) estimated $100M in June. one source, unaudited
  • Profitable? Only secondary write-ups say so; Canaan's post doesn't. not confirmed
  • Its supplier is Runware, the same as ours (Runware's case study quotes OpenArt's founder on "incredibly competitive pricing", with no number). It started as self-hosted Stable Diffusion on rented computers. one source
  • Credits: "Subscription credits do not carry over to the next month", and paid credits are lost when the plan ends (its FAQ). Its terms let it change how many credits a job costs mid-subscription. proven
  • "Unlimited" is a rotating promotion, mostly on cheap open models (FLUX, Wan). The one closed-model "unlimited" (Seedance 2.5) ran for 7 days. proven on its What's New page

Signs of how rivals do it

  • Lovart sells "Nano Banana Pro & 2 Off-peak half price", which matches Google's half-price tiers. Its night-time image pack (22:00–03:00) works out to about $0.015 a picture, below even Google's half price, so that part is a promotion or a loss.
  • Topview sells Seedance 2.5 at about $0.12–0.15 a second, against ByteDance's published $0.231. No published route explains that.
  • Higgsfield says it is "the only platform with an official ByteDance partnership" (its own blog; no ByteDance page confirms it).
  • Imagine.art's case study on Google's own site says over half its AI costs go to Google, and names a Google Cloud partner (Tridorian) that manages its credits and quota.
  • "Unlimited" almost always means a slow queue: Lovart "processed as system GPU resources become available", Topview "wait times may be lengthy", Krea unlimited only on its own in-house models, Dzine under a "Fair Usage Policy".

What remains unknown: any rival's real cost per video, its share of unused credits, and its margins; none publishes them. 6Unused credits: the data and the math

Added 30 October, from your comment: how many credits go unused, how often, and how much would have to go unused to cut prices and keep the margin. Every outside figure below was read on its own page.

In short:

  • Solorender has no data of its own yet. No outside customer has used credits. The only live purchase was your own refunded test.
  • No AI studio publishes how many credits go unused. A search across 13 found nothing, including Midjourney, Runway, Adobe Firefly, Kling, Higgsfield, Krea, OpenArt and Freepik. The closest clue: one pricing writer sees 70–80% of AI usage come from 10% of users. In other words, most people use little.
  • Long-lived prepaid value goes unused only 5–9% of the time. That's gift cards and loyalty notes in five company filings (none says whether its cards expire). Starbucks cards never expire, and the breakage it books each year is about 1.5% of what's loaded that year.
  • Removing expiry can mean more gets used. Southwest stopped its flight credits expiring in 2022. In 2024 it took back $116 million of breakage it had already counted as income on those credits.
  • The rule of thumb: to keep the same margin as a percentage, the share of credits left unused must about equal the price cut. To keep the same dollars per customer, it must be about twice the cut.
  • So: with credits that never expire, the evidence (5–9% unused on long-lived prepaid value) pays for a price cut of about 5% while holding the margin percentage. Bigger cuts (10–30%) need 11–72% unused. That's what software seats show (36–53%), and it happens where unused allowance is lost each month, which your "never expire" promise rules out.

The math: how much has to go unused to cut prices

Each plan below starts from the letter's worst case: every credit used. That gives Solo $10.87 left per customer, a 54% margin. A price cut keeps the same credits but takes less money, so the unused credits have to make up the gap. Two ways to measure "keep my margin":

  • Same margin %: the share of each payment you keep stays the same. The unused share needed is about the size of the cut.
  • Same dollars per customer: you keep the same $10.87 (Solo) per customer. The unused share needed is about twice the cut, because the cheaper price also shrinks the margin.

What this means for Solorender

  • Unused credits that never expire aren't money in the bank. A customer can come back and use them any time. They only turn into margin once the customer is truly gone. That's why the long-lived examples (5–9%, Starbucks about 1.5% a year) are far below the examples where unused allowance is lost each month (36–53%).
  • A cut of about 5% is the most the evidence supports today while keeping the margin percentage. Bigger cuts would be a bet on behaviour Solorender hasn't seen yet.
  • A note on your 2.5× rule: a price cut paid for by unused credits means a customer who uses everything gets credits at below 2.5× cost. That's a change to the rule, not a cost saving, so it's your call.
  • The cheapest way to get real numbers: once customers arrive, the database can show each month how many plan credits are still unspent after 30, 60 and 90 days, and how many sit on cancelled accounts. A few months of that replaces every borrowed figure on this page.
