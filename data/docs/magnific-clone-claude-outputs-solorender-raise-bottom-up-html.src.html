How much should Solorender raise, counted from the bottom up?

The letter asks for up to $150,000. That figure came first: on 29 September you picked "Small: under $150k" from a list of sizes, and the budget was fitted to it, with "growth money" taking whatever was left. This page does it the other way round. It prices every line on its own, adds the costs the letter doesn't have yet, sizes the growth money for a stated goal, then adds them up. Nothing in the letter has changed. It stays as it is until you pick an option below.

2Sizing the growth money for a goal

The goal: by month 12, enough customers paying at the same time that what they leave you covers your $5,000 a month plus the $62 of fixed services. After that, your pay comes from sales, which is what the letter's pay rule already promises.

How many customers that is, from the letter's section 7 (what each customer leaves after AI, card and tech costs, every credit used):

  • Studio leaves $20.21 a month → $5,062 ÷ $20.21 = 251 customers.
  • Solo leaves $10.87 a month → $5,062 ÷ $10.87 = 466 customers.

What winning them costs. Ads are the only channel with a published cost per paying customer: $57, $92 or $172 on Meta in the letter's best, middle and cautious cases. The letter's 6- and 12-month figures show how many stay. Customers are won evenly over 12 months. The money they pay along the way is counted back in. "Most cash needed at once" is the deepest the account goes, so it's the amount the growth money has to hold.

Why I picked Studio, middle case. The course is your main way to win customers for at least the next 60 days, and it comes with every plan except Solo (Studio, Max and Super Max), so the customers it brings are on Studio or bigger. I sized for Studio, the smallest of those: a Max customer leaves $38.29 a month, so fewer would be needed. The middle case is the middle of the letter's published figures. In the cautious case, ads don't pay back, so the letter's own rule stops them, and no budget should be sized for that.

What this sizing can't see, in both directions:

  • Cheaper: affiliates cost nothing until a customer pays. Creators may win customers for less than ads. No published figure says by how much, so they aren't counted.
  • Cheaper: yearly plans, now shown first on the site, collect 12 months up front. That lowers the cash needed. No published figure tells me how many customers will pick yearly, so it isn't counted.
  • Dearer: an affiliate's 25% comes out of the first 12 months, so an affiliate customer leaves less (Studio $10.21 a month), and it takes more of them to cover your pay.
  • My calculation: the letter gives how many customers stay at 6 and 12 months. I spread the losses evenly between those points, month by month. A real drop is usually steepest in month one.

3Buffer and runway: what the guides say

A buffer for surprises. Stripe's guide says set aside "5%–10% of your total budget." Two finance guides say 10–15%. 10% is where they overlap, so this page uses 10%.

How long a raise should last. Most guides say 18 months or more. The letter pays you for 12 months. After that, your pay continues only if sales cover it.

Runway advice is written for companies that live on investor money until the next round. Your plan is different: sales are meant to cover your pay from month 12. So 18 months is a safety margin for if sales come slower, not a requirement of the plan.

5Options

A · Keep$150,000

Investors get 3.0%. It fits when prices land low ($29,641 spare). With every price high it is $2,084 short, with no buffer. The letter would name the new costs and the growth-money goal, and admit the squeeze.

B · My recommendation$170,000

Investors get 3.4%. Covers every line at its high price, the new costs and the Studio goal, with $17,916 spare (more than a 10% buffer). With low prices, $49,641 is spare and goes to whichever channel sells. Founder pay stays 12 months.

C · Standard runway$200,000

Investors get 4.0%. B plus 6 more months of your pay ($30,000), matching the 18 months most guides advise. It's the safest choice if sales come slower than the plan.

D · Plan for Solo customersabout $224,000

Investors get about 4.5%. Only needed if most customers buy the $20 plan. The course points them to Studio, so I don't recommend sizing for this now.

Why B and not C: B is the amount the plan itself needs, priced line by line. C buys time in case the plan runs slow. Both are defensible. B keeps more of the company, and C is what an investor following the 18-month rule would expect.

What changes in the letter if you pick B or C: the raise amount everywhere it appears, the section 11 table, a new "Legal and accounting" line, growth money explained as a goal ("sales cover my pay by month 12") instead of a leftover, a buffer line, the key numbers panel, and 3.0% in the terms. The share copy is rebuilt the same way. If you pick A, only the new costs and the goal wording go in.

Lawyer note, from the letter's earlier research: stating terms in a widely shared letter may count as "general solicitation" under Rule 506(b). The lawyer in the Legal line would check that too.
