How We Made Take 47

This is the step-by-step for making a 60-second film with Solorender. Every stage shows both ways. Way 1 is telling Claude, which runs Solorender for you through the connection. Way 2 is clicking through the app yourself.

The page grows with the film. After each stage, I add the exact words used, what it cost and what went wrong. A stage marked Later is the plan until we reach it. This page is also the source for the video how-tos, one for each way, which get scripted once the film is finished.

Three rules for the whole film

  • Nothing paid runs without a yes. One step, one price, one yes. A yes never carries over to the next step. In Way 2, the price is on the green button, so read it before you press.
  • Build the cast before the shots. Every character, prop and place is saved once as an entity, and every keyframe is built from those names.
  • Regular, then style, then save. Every saved picture gets the style before it's saved, so each shot inherits the same look.

What the first seven shots taught us

Each of these cost real money to learn. Every one is now part of how the rest of the film gets made.

  • Place things with screen words. Write screen-left, center and screen-right, never "behind him" or "on the far side of him". In Shot 7, three pictures in a row put the lunch table in front of the dragon. The fourth, written only in screen positions, got it right the first time.
  • Edits add and remove, but they don't move things. Asked to move the table, the edit drew a second table and left the first one where it was. To move something, ask for two jobs: take it away here, add it there.
  • Keep an approved object by cutting it out, never by redrawing it. Four tries at redrawing the film camera twisted it, fused it to the dragon, invented a new design or flipped it. Cutting the approved camera out of its picture and pasting it in kept it exact.
  • A camera must point at what it films. You caught a camera aimed off to the side: "the camera needs to be pointed at the dragon."
  • Start a clip just before the action. A clip continues the pose it's handed, so the megaphone starts by Dottie's knee, not at her mouth.
  • When the ending is the joke, give the clip an end picture. Shot 5's glasses move only happened once the clip had to land on a picture of it.
  • A saved entity speaks twice. Its picture and its description both go to the model. The crane kept coming back until both were fixed.
  • Painted keyframes carry the look. A clip made from names alone, with no start picture, came out darker and glossier, more like a video game. Your note: "it feels like the look won't match the other clips."
  • Give every character small life. Never write "does not move". A Grok clip told that froze Dottie like a still picture, and the dragon shot fire instead of sneezing.
  • Words shape bodies. "Heavy" and "barrel-chested" made the dragon fat ("He looks fat"). "Huge, powerful and muscular" keeps him right.
  • Feet and American spelling in every prompt: "let's not use European."

Check for drift

Put each keyframe beside your hero and go down this list. Fix problems before any clip is made, because a picture redo is far cheaper than a clip redo.

  • Dottie: same face, hair, glasses and cardigan.
  • Dragon: same soft face, scales, harness and wings.
  • Props and place: same brass megaphone, chair and set.
  • Hands: what is each hand touching?
  • Story: would a stranger understand the moment at a glance?
  • Angle: is it a real change from the last shot, not a zoom?
  • Shape: wide, not square.
  • Where things are: in a start-and-end pair, does every object sit in the same place in both? An object that moves with no cause slides across the clip.

Clips get their own check before you see them: a strip of frames at two to four a second, a check for cuts, loudness, and a speech check. You still judge playback, and you've caught things the strips missed, like the dragon stepping forward in the first two seconds of a Shot 7 take.

Shot 7: one clip with cuts, from saved names

Your idea: describe one scene from your saved names, with timed cuts between camera angles. The prompt follows two guides in your docs folder. The Nexus skill gives the layout: one block per saved name saying what it controls, a scene paragraph, a timed list of hard cuts, then sound. The Absurd Filmmaking Skill gives the rules: name the endpoint, play behavior instead of labels ("chin up, chest out", not "proud"), and give every move weight.

  • The model: Kling 4.0 · Entities builds a clip from your @names, up to 30 seconds with sound. Lines like "CUT 2 (00:03 to 00:06)" gave real cuts at 3.04 and 6.08 seconds.

  • Take 1 (1931): the table jumped from the side to the center between cuts, and he walked forward instead of backing up, because my prompt said both "side-on" and "head-on".
