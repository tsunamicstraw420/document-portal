Tall Cut

A way to turn any wide video into a full-screen vertical Short, the same way we made the tall version of Grandma's Story. A tall window slides over the original picture and keeps each person's face filling the phone. It's free and runs on the Mac. It doesn't invent anything new.

The old way

What the current Shorts do

Today's Shorts put the whole wide picture across the middle of the phone, 1080 × 608. The top and bottom are filled with a blurred, darkened copy of the same picture. One headline stays up for the whole clip, and the captions sit in the blur under the picture.

From the frames of SHORT_01_hook and SHORT_05_paola, this is what I see wrong with it:

  • The real picture covers only about a third of the screen. The rest is blur.
  • Faces are small. Ruby's face is about a sixth of the screen's width (measured: 17–20%). In the tall cut it's 52–64%.
  • It reads as a TV clip reposted to a phone, not a video made for the phone.
  • Nothing about the framing changes with the story. Every shot gets the same treatment.

Correction: an earlier version of this page said you had never complained about these Shorts, because nothing was in your notes. That was wrong. You did complain in chats and were talked into blur-fill as a compromise when you were new, and you were never pleased with it. Blur-fill is now retired.

The method

How a tall cut is made

  1. Start from the clean wide master

    The version with no captions burned in. For the Podcast that's the master in _READY_TO_UPLOAD, never the captioned YouTube file.

  2. Find every cut, to the exact frame

    The tool compares each frame with the one before. It tells hard cuts from the Podcast's half-second zoom-blur transitions. On Grandma's Story the window once moved 0.2 s late at a cut because the timing sheet was a few frames off, so now the real cuts are always re-found in the video itself.

  3. Find who is in each shot

    The Mac's built-in face finder (Apple Vision) looks at 5 frames a second. It also follows each person through the shot and notices whose lips move. Animals, creatures and scenery get a "where the eye goes" guess, marked for a look by eye.

  4. Place the tall window shot by shot

    The window holds still for the whole shot and jumps on the exact cut frame. It only glides when someone walks across, when a couple doesn't fit, or inside a blur transition. Any shot can be moved by hand before rendering.

  5. Enlarge to 1080 × 1920 for free

    Plain enlarging with a light sharpen. Topaz was tested on Grandma's Story and made no visible difference.

  6. Run the checks, then look

    Numbers first (below), then the sheets by eye: the whole video at 2 frames a second, every cut close up, and feet and ground close up.

  7. Put the words on last

    Captions and titles follow the Motion Text Playbook: under the chin, never on a face, three short lines a page. The tool saves where every face sits so the text can avoid them.

Proposal · solorender clip tool · nothing built

"Make vertical"

The same method as a button on any wide clip: a customer's upload, a 16:9 generated clip, or a Video editor export. solorender shows the planned tall framing shot by shot, the customer can drag any window, and the tall copy lands in their Library. The same checks run on it automatically.

Recommended · runs in the customer's browser
Free

Works like "Grab a frame" and the Video editor export, which already run on the customer's own computer and cost us nothing. Google's MediaPipe face finder runs inside a web page (checked on its official guide). The enlarging and saving reuse the editor's export engine.

Our real cost is storage only. A 47-second tall clip is about 50 MB, which is about $0.0008 a month on R2. At 2.5× that rounds to 0 credits. It counts against the customer's storage limit like any other file.

Fallback · on our render server
≈ 4 cr / min

Measured on the Mac: about 1,600 processor-seconds per minute of video. On a Render.com 1-processor worker ($25 a month, from Render's pricing page), that's about $0.015 a minute, × 2.5 = 4 credits per started minute.

It's slow: about 27 minutes of waiting per minute of video on one processor. The monthly fee is charged even when nobody uses it. The current $7 worker is too small and is busy assembling films.

What I'd build (browser route)

  • A "Make vertical" button on clip pages, the Studio clip player, and the Video editor's export panel ("also make a tall copy").
  • In the browser: find the cuts, find the faces, and place the window using the same rules as the Mac tool.
  • A review screen with one picture per shot and a window the customer can drag.
  • Render, run the automatic checks, and save to the Library as a new item linked to the original.
  • A help article and a /tools page for search ("vertical video converter").

Unproven until tested

  • How well MediaPipe finds illustrated or cartoon faces. Many solorender clips are illustrated.
  • Spotting who's talking needs Google's lip-tracking version (Face Landmarker), not tested.
  • Safari: the editor export already has limits there (some blur effects, memory with two exports at once).
