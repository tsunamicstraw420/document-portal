The 6 rules at a glance:
bullet Rule 1: Cut trades at FTA

bullet Rule 2: Establish a time-based stop loss

bullet Rule 3: Cut based on volume trends

The 3 Traps of 'Set and Forget'
Most traders run a 'set and forget' process without any trade management: enter the trade, then wait to
see if it's a winner or loser. This approach leads to 3 traps — even a trader with real edge can still lose
money.

Trap 1 — Winning trade → loser.
Price travels 90% to your target. Out of nowhere it reverses and goes all the way to your full stop loss.
A winning trade turned into a loser.

Trap 2 — Leaving money on the table.
The trade steamrolls to your target and you exit. Later, the price has continued a further 20% past your
target. You caught a winner but captured less than the market offered.

Trap 3 — Full hit on losing trades.
You regret the entry almost immediately. Despite clear warning signs, you watch it travel straight to
stop loss and lose the maximum amount.
The 6 rules below avoid these traps. Each rule is either mechanical (binary, triggered by defined
conditions — usable even by beginners) or discretionary (subjective patterns in the charts — better
for intermediate traders).
Examples below use breakout trades, altcoins on the 1-minute timeframe, and 1R targets — but the
frameworks apply across strategies and timeframes.
Rule 1 — Cut Trades at FTA
Mechanical · targets Trap 3 (Full hit on losing trades)
If after entering a position:
bullet Price barely goes onside (in profit)

bullet Closes through the FTA (First Trouble Area — the level on the way to the stop loss)

bullet Cut the trade with a −0.5R loss (rather than a full −1R)

FTA is defined as the price level at 0.5× your risk distance — on a 1R trade, that's −0.5R. The candle
must close beyond that level, not just wick through it.
Why 0.5R specifically? Across a large sample, the author's team found that cutting at a 0.5R-offside
candle close is expected-value positive (EV+). Track this in your own journal to verify on your data.
  Summary: If price barely goes onside, then closes through the FTA, cut the trade for −0.5R instead of
  taking the full −1R.


Rule 2 — Establish a Time-Based Stop Loss
Mechanical · targets Trap 3
The FTA rule catches trades that move against you in price. But some trades enter a limbo zone — not
hitting stop, not approaching target, just sitting there. A time-based stop frees up your capital, attention,
and emotional energy.
Steps:
bullet Step 1: Track entry time, exit time, duration, and winner/loser flag.

bullet Step 2: Compute your average winning trade duration (spreadsheet:
      =AVERAGEIF(W6:W1000,"W",Z6:Z1000)*60).
bullet Step 3: Multiply that average by 2.

bullet Step 4: That number is your time-based stop. Cut trades that exceed it.

Example: if your average winner is 18 minutes, your time stop is 36 minutes. Past that, the trade is
unlikely to be a winner.
Why 2×? Across 10,000+ trades from the author and students, this is the point where trades tend to be
EV−. Validate it on your own data.
  Summary: If you're stuck in a trade for roughly 2× your average winning duration, cut it.


Rule 3 — Cut Based on Volume Trends
Discretionary · targets Traps 2 and 3
Before a breakout, grinding price action with steadily increasing volume is the strongest continuation
signal. Grinding price action with volume that drops off after your entry is a warning sign — even if
the price still looks clean.
Volume tapering tells you participation is drying up; moves without participation tend to stall or reverse.
Process:
bullet Enter breakout trade (grinding price action + volume increasing into the level)

bullet Watch for the warning sign: volume suddenly drops off after your entry

bullet Consider cutting the trade early

  This rule is discretionary, not mechanical. Use carefully without a proven statistical edge.


Rule 4 — The 0.9R Rule (Evolving R)
Mechanical · targets Trap 1 (Winning trade → loser)
Your risk:reward in a trade is not static — it evolves as price moves:
bullet As price moves in your favour, reward shrinks and risk grows.

bullet As price moves against you, reward grows and risk shrinks.

The 'Grab the Mouse' test. 20 minutes into a 1-minute breakout, someone reaches over, grabs your
mouse, and closes your position. Would you frantically re-enter the same trade — same stop, same
target — or would you walk away?
At entry, you had a 1:1 trade (−1R risk for +1R reward). By the time price reaches +0.9R, your
remaining risk is −1.9R for a remaining reward of +0.1R — a 19:1 risk-to-reward against you. Almost no
one would re-enter that.
The rule: When price reaches +0.9R in your favour, move your stop to +0.1R in your favour
(approximately breakeven after fees and slippage). This eliminates the scenario where a trade reaches
90% of target and then reverses to a full stop loss. You can set this up visually with the Fibonacci tool
on your charts.
  Summary: At +0.9R, move stop to +0.1R. Lock in (near-)breakeven on trades that have done most of
  the work.
Rule 5 — Use the Orderbook to Extend TP
Discretionary, advanced · targets Trap 2 (Leaving money on the table)
For breakout trades, when you understand liquidity you can let winners run further than your initial
target.
Background. Orderflow is the study of price movement in markets. The orderbook is the live list of all
limit orders waiting to fill at each price level — passive limit sells above price, passive limit buys below.
Price moves from one stack of orders to the next; some stacks are thick (lots of resistance), some are
thin (easy to slice through).
Bookmap visualises three things at once:
bullet Volume bubbles — green = aggressive market buys, red = aggressive market sells. Bigger
     bubble = bigger order.
bullet Heat map — resting limit orders by colour. Dark/black = thin; blue → yellow → orange → red =
     progressively thicker.
bullet Order book depth on the right-side panel.

4 orderbook signals to extend take profit:
bullet Clusters of green volume bubbles stacking in the direction of the move

bullet Limit orders trailing behind price, building support as it moves

bullet Thin order book ahead of price (dark/blue colours, few horizontal lines)

bullet Increasing volume over time, confirming growing participation

  When you see these signals aligning, you can hold past your original 1R target instead of
  automatically exiting.


Rule 6 — Establish Patterns for Extending TP
Discretionary · targets Trap 2
There are 3 key patterns. When all 3 align on a breakout trade, you can discretionarily extend your take
profit:
bullet Pattern 1 — Onside right away. The trade went straight into profit with no fuss after entry.

bullet Pattern 2 — Grinding to TP. A clean grind toward target — minimal criss-crossing or reverting to
     entry level. Consistent movement toward TP.
bullet Pattern 3 — High-quality setup. Identified by 4 variables (see below).

4 variables of a high-quality breakout setup:
bullet Slow, grindy staircase price action

bullet Clearly increasing volume

bullet Minimal moving-average crossovers

bullet Clean trending price action on the Left Hand Side
  Summary: When all 3 patterns align (onside right away, clean grind, high-quality setup), extend the
  take profit past 1R.


Putting It All Together
The six rules pair up against the three traps:
bullet Trap 1 (winner → loser): Rule 4 (0.9R / Evolving R)

bullet Trap 2 (money on the table): Rules 3, 5, 6 (volume, orderbook, patterns)

bullet Trap 3 (full hit on losers): Rules 1, 2 (FTA, time-based stop)

Mechanical rules (1, 2, 4) are binary and usable immediately — even by beginners. Discretionary rules
(3, 5, 6) require pattern recognition and market experience — they reward intermediate traders who
track results carefully.
These rules compound when you track them in a journal (logging FTA cuts, durations, +0.9R
outcomes, orderbook signals, and pattern alignments) so you can verify each rule is EV+ on your data
— not just on faith.
